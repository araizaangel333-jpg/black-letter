## Facts
Question (15 points, 40 minutes suggested). Rosa runs a landscaping company in Plano. On March 3, 2026 she called Trailer Depot, a dealer, and orally ordered three standard utility trailers at $4,000 each and one custom trailer with a built-in water tank and her company's logo painted on both sides, at $9,000, for delivery on April 15 with payment on delivery. They agreed orally that any dispute would have to be brought within one year and that the trailers carried a two-year warranty against defects in materials.

On March 6 Trailer Depot emailed Rosa a signed confirmation listing the four trailers, the prices, the delivery date, the warranty and the one-year suit clause. Rosa read it and did not reply. Trailer Depot ordered the tank and began fabricating the custom trailer on March 20. On April 1 Rosa emailed from her company account, signed with her name and title: Trailer Depot, I have to cancel the trailer order I placed with you last month; my budget was cut. Trailer Depot delivered all four trailers on April 15; Rosa refused them.

Trailer Depot finished the custom trailer and could not resell it. It sues Rosa on June 1, 2030 for the price of the custom trailer and damages on the three standard trailers. Rosa raises the statute of frauds and the statute of limitations.

## Call of the question
(a) Is the oral contract enforceable against Rosa, and as to which trailers? Discuss fully. (9 points)
(b) Is the suit timely? Discuss, including the effect of the one-year clause and whether the limitations period runs from the April 1 email or from April 15. (6 points)

## Model answer
(a) Enforceability. The trailers are goods (§ 2-105) and the contract exceeds $500, so § 2-201 applies; the UCC governs with the common law filling gaps (§ 1-103(b)).

First, the writing. Rosa's April 1 email is a signed writing (electronic signature suffices) by the party to be charged, and it indicates that a contract was made: it refers to the trailer order I placed. The difficulty is quantity: § 2-201(1) enforces only to the quantity shown, and the email names no number. Trailer Depot will argue the reference to the order identifies the specific four trailers; Rosa will argue the email states no quantity and so fails. Courts split on whether a writing may incorporate the quantity by reference to an identified order; the better reading, given that the statute requires the quantity to appear, is that the email alone is insufficient but may be read with the March 6 confirmation it refers to.

Second, the merchant's confirmation (§ 2-201(2)). Trailer Depot's March 6 email is a writing in confirmation sufficient against the sender, received by Rosa, who read it and did not object within ten days. The exception requires both parties to be merchants (§ 2-104). Trailer Depot is. Rosa is a landscaper, not a trailer dealer, but a person who by occupation holds herself out as having knowledge peculiar to the practices involved, or who regularly deals in goods of the kind, is a merchant; a business owner buying equipment for her business is treated as a merchant for § 2-201(2) purposes by most courts (the business-person reading of the definition), though some courts limit the term to people who deal in goods of that kind. If Rosa is a merchant, the confirmation satisfies the statute as to all four trailers and the quantity problem disappears. Rosa's best argument is that she is a merchant in landscaping services, not in trailers, and the Code's comment supports a narrower reading for some sections; the exam answer should present both and conclude that most courts would treat her as a merchant for this purpose.

Third, specially manufactured goods (§ 2-201(3)(a)). The custom trailer with a water tank and Rosa's logo is not suitable for sale to others in the ordinary course, and Trailer Depot made a substantial beginning (ordered the tank, began fabrication on March 20) before the April 1 repudiation. That exception enforces the contract as to the custom trailer only; the three standard trailers are stock goods and the exception does not reach them (divisibility). Rosa will argue that ordering a tank and starting fabrication is not a substantial beginning; the facts say fabrication had begun two weeks before the cancellation, which is enough.

Fourth, admission and part performance. If Rosa admits the order in her pleadings or testimony, the contract is enforceable to the admitted quantity (§ 2-201(3)(b)); the April 1 email reads like an admission. No payment was made and no goods were accepted, so § 2-201(3)(c) does not apply.

Conclusion on (a): the contract is enforceable as to the custom trailer under § 2-201(3)(a) regardless of Rosa's merchant status; as to all four trailers if Rosa is a merchant (confirmation) or if the April 1 email, read with the confirmation, supplies the quantity.

(b) Timeliness. Section 2-725(1) gives four years from accrual and allows the parties to reduce the period to not less than one year; the oral one-year clause is within the permitted range, but it is part of the oral agreement and must itself survive the statute of frauds analysis above; if the contract is enforceable, the clause is enforceable, and it bars a suit filed on June 1, 2030 in any event. If the clause does not apply, the four-year period controls. Accrual: the April 1 email was a repudiation (Hochster; § 2-610). If the breach occurred on April 1, 2026, four years expired April 1, 2030 and the suit is late. If the breach occurred when Rosa refused the goods on April 15, 2026, four years expired April 15, 2030 and the suit is still late. Either way the June 1, 2030 filing is untimely; the exam should nonetheless analyze both accrual dates because on different numbers the choice would decide the case. (A seller who awaits performance after repudiation can argue the clock starts at the performance date; courts divide.)

Conclusion on (b): barred, under the one-year clause if the contract is enforceable, and under the four-year period on either accrual date.

## Common mistakes
- Skipping the merchant analysis for the buyer and assuming the confirmation works.
- Applying the specially manufactured goods exception to the whole order.
- Forgetting that a contractual limitation clause can shorten to one year but not extend.
- Choosing one accrual date without discussing the other.
