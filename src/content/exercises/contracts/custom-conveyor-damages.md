## Facts
Question (14 points, 40 minutes suggested). Bluebonnet Packaging buys a custom conveyor system from Lonestar Machinery for $240,000 under a written contract that promises delivery and installation by March 1. The contract contains a clause: seller shall not be liable for consequential damages; for each day of delay in delivery seller shall pay buyer $500 as liquidated damages. When the contract was signed in November, Bluebonnet's purchasing manager told Lonestar's salesman that the line was for a new packaging operation; he did not mention any particular customer. In January Bluebonnet signed a one-year contract with a Dallas grocery chain worth $600,000, with a fixed profit margin of 15 percent, to begin March 15, and emailed Lonestar that the conveyor had to be running by March 1 for a major customer.

Lonestar delivered and installed on April 10, forty days late; the conveyor was otherwise perfect. In March a rental company offered Bluebonnet a used conveyor for $9,000 for the month, which Bluebonnet refused because it would have needed two days of adjustments. The grocery chain cancelled on March 20 under a clause allowing cancellation if operations were not running by March 15. Bluebonnet's fixed costs during the forty days continued; it had no other use for the idle workers, whose wages for the period were $30,000.

## Call of the question
What damages can Bluebonnet recover from Lonestar? Discuss fully, including the effect of the contract clause.

## Model answer
Governing law and breach. The conveyor is goods; UCC Article 2 applies. Lonestar breached by delivering forty days late; the goods conformed, so the claim is for damages for accepted goods with a delay in delivery (§ 2-714) plus incidental and consequential damages (§ 2-715), subject to the contract's limitation clauses (§§ 2-718, 2-719).

Direct damages. For delay in delivering conforming goods, the buyer's direct loss is the value of use of the goods during the delay, commonly measured by the reasonable rental value of a substitute: here about $12,000 for forty days at the rental company's $9,000-a-month rate. This is recoverable as the loss resulting in the ordinary course from the breach.

Consequential damages: the lost grocery contract. Under § 2-715(2)(a), consequential damages include loss resulting from requirements and needs of which the seller at the time of contracting had reason to know and which could not reasonably be prevented by cover or otherwise. This is Hadley in statutory form: the measuring moment is contracting, November. At that time Lonestar knew only that the conveyor was for a new packaging operation, which makes some lost business a natural consequence of delay but does not disclose a $600,000 fixed-margin contract conditioned on a March 15 start. The January email came after contracting and cannot expand the risk Lonestar assumed when it priced the deal. Bluebonnet will argue that a seller of production equipment to a packaging company has reason to know that delay costs the buyer customers, and that the general nature of the loss, not its precise amount, is what must be foreseeable; Lonestar will argue that a cancellable contract with a hard start date is a special circumstance of the kind Hadley requires to be communicated. The better answer: some lost profits from the idle line are foreseeable in general, but the loss of this particular contract, with its cancellation clause, was not within Lonestar's contemplation in November. If a court disagrees, the lost profit is $90,000 (15 percent of $600,000), which is reasonably certain because the customer, price and margin were fixed, satisfying § 352.

Avoidability. Section 2-715(2)(a) and § 350 deny consequential loss that could reasonably have been prevented. Bluebonnet refused a $9,000 rental that would have had the line running by about March 3 after two days of adjustment, well before the March 15 deadline. Refusing a cheap, available substitute that would have saved the contract is a failure to mitigate; the recoverable consequential loss is reduced to what mitigation would not have prevented, which here is close to nothing beyond the rental cost. Lonestar bears the burden of proving the failure to mitigate, and the facts give it the proof. The idle wages ($30,000) are likewise a loss that the rental would have avoided; absent mitigation they are recoverable only to the extent they were a natural consequence of the delay and not avoidable.

The clause. Section 2-719(3) allows exclusion of consequential damages unless unconscionable; between businesses for commercial loss the exclusion is enforceable, so even a foreseeable lost profit would be barred. The $500-a-day liquidated damages term is enforceable under § 2-718(1) if reasonable in light of anticipated harm and difficulty of proof; $500 a day against a rental value of roughly $300 a day is within reason, so the clause likely governs and fixes delay damages at $20,000 (40 days). Bluebonnet will argue the clause is a penalty or that the exclusion fails of its essential purpose (§ 2-719(2)); neither is strong on these facts, because the liquidated sum is a fair estimate and the buyer keeps a remedy.

Conclusion. Most likely recovery: $20,000 in liquidated damages for the forty-day delay, which displaces the rental-value measure; no consequential damages, because the lost contract was not foreseeable at contracting, was avoidable by the $9,000 rental, and is excluded by a valid clause. Without the clause, the analysis would yield about $12,000 direct damages and at most a small unavoidable consequential loss.

## Common mistakes
- Measuring foreseeability at the time of breach or of the January email instead of at contracting.
- Awarding the $600,000 contract price instead of the $90,000 profit.
- Ignoring the rental offer, the fact the problem was built around.
- Treating the consequential-damages exclusion as automatically unconscionable.
