## Facts
Question (8 points, 25 minutes suggested). Alamo Builders is preparing a bid for a public library. On the morning bids are due it receives roofing bids of $118,000, $121,000 and, from Summit Roofing, $96,000 by phone. The Summit estimator says: that is our number, use it. Alamo lists Summit in its bid, which is $14,000 below the next bidder, and wins the award the same afternoon. Alamo's project manager, before calling Summit, calls the two other roofers to ask whether they can beat $96,000; both decline. Eight days later Alamo calls Summit to confirm. Summit says its estimator omitted the cost of the metal flashing and will do the job only for $117,000. Alamo hires the $118,000 roofer and sues Summit for $22,000.

## Call of the question
Is Summit liable, and for how much? Discuss fully.

## Model answer
Issue. Whether Summit's $96,000 bid became irrevocable when Alamo used it, and whether Alamo's conduct forfeited that protection.

Rule. A bid is an offer, revocable at any time before acceptance unless the offeree gave consideration for an option (Dickinson v. Dodds) or the offer is a merchant's firm offer for goods (UCC § 2-205), which does not cover roofing services. Under R.2d § 87(2) and Drennan v. Star Paving, an offer that the offeror should reasonably expect to induce substantial reliance before acceptance, and which does induce it, is binding as an option to the extent necessary to avoid injustice. The protection is lost if the general bid-shops the sub's price, delays acceptance beyond a reasonable time, or had reason to know the bid was a mistake.

Application. Summit should have expected reliance: its estimator said use it, and subs know generals build bids from their numbers. Alamo relied by listing Summit and pricing its bid accordingly, and it won. The injustice is concrete: Alamo is bound to the library at a price computed on $96,000.

The limitations are the real fight. Bid shopping: Alamo called the other roofers to ask if they could beat Summit's price. Drennan protects the general only if it treats the bid as an irrevocable option it will accept, not as a floor for further negotiation; using the bid to extract lower prices from others is exactly the conduct the rule denies protection to. Summit's strongest argument is that Alamo forfeited the option the moment it shopped. Alamo will answer that the calls produced nothing and that it promptly came back to Summit; some courts ask whether the shopping harmed the sub, others treat any shopping as disqualifying. Delay: eight days after award is within a reasonable time for a public project, where the general must wait for the award and contract before committing to subs; no forfeiture there. Known mistake: $96,000 against $118,000 and $121,000 is about 19 percent below the next bid; a gap of that size, without more, is within the ordinary spread of roofing bids, so Alamo did not have reason to know of a mistake, unlike a bid at a third of the others.

Remedy. If Summit is bound, Alamo recovers the difference between the bid and the reasonable cost of replacement roofing, $22,000, the expectation measure Drennan applied; Alamo need not accept Summit's $117,000 because the option was for $96,000. Summit cannot claim Alamo owed it the job before acceptance; the asymmetry is the doctrine's known cost.

Conclusion. Summit is liable for $22,000 unless the court treats Alamo's calls to the other roofers as bid shopping; because those calls sought to beat Summit's price rather than to confirm it, a court applying Drennan's limits strictly would deny recovery, and the answer should say that the shopping is the likely dispositive issue.

## Common mistakes
- Treating the bid as a firm offer under the UCC; roofing is services.
- Missing the calls to the other roofers, the one fact the problem was built around.
- Measuring damages as Summit's revised price minus its bid instead of the replacement cost minus the bid.
