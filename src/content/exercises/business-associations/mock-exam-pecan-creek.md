## Facts
Part 1 of 2 (50 points, 90 minutes suggested, 1,200-word limit). Pecan Creek Logistics, Inc. is a Texas corporation based in Fort Worth with 2,100 employees, 38 shareholders and no listed shares; its certificate of formation has the usual clause eliminating directors' monetary liability to the fullest extent Texas law allows, and it has not elected into any optional governance statute. The board has seven members: Dolores Ibarra, chief executive and 18 percent shareholder; Hank Pruitt, chief financial officer; Lupe Castañeda, the founder's daughter, a 22 percent shareholder who lives in Austin and does not work in the business; and four outside directors, among them Marcus Bell, Ibarra's brother-in-law, whose brokerage earns about $400,000 a year placing the company's truck insurance, and Renata Okafor, a retired accountant who chairs the audit committee with Bell and one other outside director.

The Laredo terminal. In March the board approved the sale of the company's Laredo cross-dock terminal to Ibarra Family Holdings, LP, a partnership owned by Ibarra and her siblings, for $9 million. Ibarra presented the deal on a 25-minute videoconference using a two-page summary and an appraisal of $9.2 million from a firm she had selected and paid. She stated that she was interested in the buyer and abstained from the vote; the other six directors voted yes, Bell included. Nobody asked whether anyone else had shown interest. In fact, a competitor had emailed Ibarra in December offering $11.5 million for the terminal; she never forwarded the email to the board or to Pruitt.

The driver logs. Since the previous summer, the company's compliance officer has sent the audit committee a quarterly report. Each of the last five reports flagged that drivers at the Houston yard were being pressured to falsify hours-of-service logs. The committee minutes record the reports as noted; no director asked management for a plan, no outside review was ordered, and the yard manager who ran the practice was promoted. In August a Pecan Creek driver who had been on duty for nineteen hours caused a fatal crash on I-45. Federal regulators fined the company $4 million, the insurer reserved its rights, and the company's largest customer suspended its contract.

Lupe Castañeda has asked you what can be done about the directors.

## Call of the question
You are outside counsel to Lupe Castañeda. In no more than 1,200 words, assess (a) the claims that can be brought against the directors over the Laredo sale and the driver logs, (b) the defenses the directors will raise, including the business judgment rule and the certificate's exculpation clause, and how each claim fares against them, and (c) the procedural route and the practical remedies. Where the outcome turns on a fact you do not have, say which fact and why it matters.

## Model answer
Conclusion first. Lupe has a strong loyalty claim over the Laredo sale, a credible bad-faith oversight claim over the driver logs, and a weaker care claim that the exculpation clause reduces to a procedural footnote. Both strong claims belong to the corporation, so they must be brought derivatively after a written demand, and the practical remedy is rescission or disgorgement on the terminal plus recovery of the fine and the lost contract from the directors who consciously ignored the logs.

Actors and duties. Ibarra wears three hats (officer, director, 18 percent shareholder) and a fourth as a partner of the buyer. Bell is a director and, through his brokerage, a paid vendor and a relative of the CEO. Okafor and the other outsiders are directors only. Directors and officers owe the corporation and its shareholders duties of care and loyalty; Lupe, as a shareholder, is owed those duties but does not owe any to the others. Creditors, the insurer and the customer have only their contracts.

Laredo: loyalty. A sale of corporate property to a partnership the CEO owns is a classic self-dealing transaction. Texas lets an interested transaction stand if the material facts of the interest and of the transaction are disclosed to the board and a majority of disinterested directors approve it in good faith, if disinterested shareholders approve it, or if it is fair to the corporation when approved (TBOC § 21.418). Ibarra disclosed her interest, but she withheld a material fact: a written offer of $11.5 million, 28 percent above the price she asked the board to accept. Approval without the material facts does not cleanse, so the burden shifts to Ibarra to prove entire fairness, fair dealing and fair price. A price $2.5 million below a documented competing bid, set with an appraisal commissioned by the interested party, fails both prongs. Bell's vote does not count as disinterested approval either: a brother-in-law earning $400,000 a year from the company is not independent of its CEO under any exchange-style test, which leaves at most four disinterested votes, and all four were uninformed.

Laredo: care. The approving directors can be sued for approving the sale on a 25-minute call with a two-page summary and an appraisal chosen by the seller. Van Gorkom is the template: directors who approve a major asset sale without reading the documents, questioning the valuation or asking about alternatives are not informed. Texas lets directors rely in good faith on reports of officers and experts (§ 3.102), but reliance must be reasonable, and reliance on a valuation procured by the one person with a conflict is not. The claim is real but, as discussed below, the exculpation clause means it yields no money.

The driver logs: oversight. Directors do not insure against corporate misconduct; under Caremark and Disney they are liable only for a good-faith failure to try, which means either no reporting system at all or conscious disregard of red flags the system produced. Here the system existed and worked: five consecutive quarterly reports put falsified logs in front of the audit committee. The committee did nothing except write the word noted, and the person responsible was promoted. That is not poor process of the Disney kind; it is a sustained decision not to act on known illegality that threatened human life, the pattern the SEC used to charge the outside directors of DHB Industries. Okafor, Bell and the third committee member are most exposed; the full board is exposed to the extent the reports or the promotion reached it. The $4 million fine, the insurance dispute and the suspended contract are the damages, subject to proof that a timely response would have avoided the crash.

Business judgment rule. The rule presumes that directors acted on an informed basis, in good faith and in the honest belief that the decision served the corporation, and a court will not second-guess a decision that fits that description. Ibarra cannot claim it for Laredo, because the rule never covers a self-interested transaction; the other directors lose it for Laredo because they were not informed; and the audit committee loses it for the logs because inaction in the face of known red flags is not a business judgment but the absence of one. Had Pecan Creek been listed or had it elected into the 2025 Texas statute (§ 21.419), Lupe would face a codified presumption that, as written, requires pleading with particularity and proving conduct beyond ordinary breach, such as fraud, intentional misconduct or a knowing violation of law. Even then, concealing a competing offer and consciously tolerating falsified safety logs are the kind of facts the statute leaves actionable; the main effect would be on the care claim, which would disappear entirely.

Exculpation and insurance. The certificate's clause, authorized by § 7.001, eliminates monetary liability for breaches of the duty of care, so the Van Gorkom-style claim produces no damages, only a finding that supports equitable relief. The clause cannot reach breaches of loyalty, acts not in good faith, or transactions from which a director received an improper personal benefit, so Ibarra's exposure on Laredo and the committee's exposure on the logs survive. D&O insurance will defend the outsiders but typically excludes deliberate dishonesty and personal profit, which again isolates Ibarra.

Procedure. Both injuries fall on the corporation, so the suit is derivative. Texas requires a written demand describing the act and a 90-day wait unless irreparable injury would result (§ 21.553); the board may refer the demand to independent and disinterested directors, whose good-faith determination to reject it, if truly independent and informed, ends the suit (§ 21.554). Bell cannot serve on such a committee; Okafor is conflicted on the logs; Pecan Creek may need to add directors to get a credible review. Lupe's 22 percent clears any ownership threshold the company could adopt. Missing facts that matter: whether the committee ever reported the logs to the full board, whether the appraisal firm knew of the competing offer, and what the insurer's reservation covers.

Remedies and advice. On Laredo, rescission of the sale or Ibarra's disgorgement of the $2.5 million difference; on the logs, damages for the fine and the lost contract against the committee members, plus governance orders. The board should have formed a special committee of independent directors with its own appraiser, required Ibarra to disclose every expression of interest, and treated the first flagged report as a crisis: an outside investigation, discipline, and a written response the minutes could show.

## Common mistakes
- Forgetting that Ibarra's abstention does not cleanse anything when the directors who voted lacked a material fact.
- Treating the exculpation clause as a shield for loyalty and bad faith; it reaches only care.
- Skipping the demand requirement and the independence problem, which is where the professor's prior exam put half the points.
- Analyzing Disney as if any poor process were bad faith; the oversight claim works here because of fourteen months of known red flags, not because the meetings were short.
