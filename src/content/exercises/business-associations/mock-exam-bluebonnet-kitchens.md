## Facts
Part 2 of 2 (50 points, 90 minutes suggested; three questions with the points shown). Bluebonnet Mobile Kitchens, LLC is a Texas limited liability company that runs four food trucks in Fort Worth. It has three members: Ana Villarreal (60 percent, the sole manager), Tomás Reyes (25 percent, who runs two of the trucks) and Priya Natarajan (15 percent, a passive investor who is a Mexican citizen living in Monterrey). The company agreement names Ana as manager, requires Tomás's written consent for any contract over $25,000, and says nothing about distributions, transfers of interests or buyouts.

Ana hired Rico Salas as operations lead. When Ana introduced Rico to the owner of Lone Star Commissary she said: this is the man who runs our operations, talk to him about kitchen space. Over the following year Rico signed two supply contracts with the commissary, each under $10,000, which the company performed. In February Rico signed a three-year lease of commissary kitchen space at $40,000 a year. Ana found out within a week, said nothing, and the trucks used the kitchen for four months; in June, when a cheaper space became available, Ana told the commissary that Rico had no authority and the lease was void.

In May, Rico drove a company truck to a catering event. Fifteen miles before the venue he left the route to pick up his dry cleaning, and while leaving the cleaner's parking lot he hit a cyclist, who suffered serious injuries.

Bluebonnet has $5,000 in its bank account. Its liability policy lapsed in March for nonpayment. The four trucks are titled in Ana's name, though the company uses and maintains them. Ana pays her apartment rent from the company account and reimburses it when she can; the ledger shows $38,000 of such advances outstanding. When the commissary owner once asked about the LLC, Ana said the LLC is just paperwork, you are dealing with me.

Meanwhile, Priya has received no distributions in two years while Ana has drawn a $150,000 salary each year; Tomás has an offer from a competitor to buy his 25 percent and wants to accept it.

## Call of the question
Question A (20 points). Is Bluebonnet bound by the lease? Is Bluebonnet liable to the cyclist? Explain each step and say who can sue whom.

Question B (15 points). The commissary (on the lease) and the cyclist (on the injury) each want to hold Ana personally liable. Assess each creditor's chances under Texas law, name the fact that would change your answer, and say what Ana should have done differently.

Question C (15 points). What rights do Priya and Tomás have under the default rules, and what should the company agreement have said? Include anything about Priya's situation that affects the choice of entity.

## Model answer
Question A: the lease. The agreement comes first: Rico's actual authority stops at $25,000 without Tomás's written consent, and a $120,000 lease exceeds it, so there is no actual authority, express or implied. Apparent authority is different: it rests on the principal's manifestations to the third party, never on the agent's own claims (Highland Capital). Ana, the manager, told the commissary that Rico runs operations and should be consulted about kitchen space, and then let Rico sign two contracts with the same landlord that the company honored. A landlord who hears that and sees that may reasonably believe the operations lead can sign for kitchen space. The commissary will not have known of the internal $25,000 limit, and Ames cuts the other way only when the earlier dealings were of a different kind: here the prior contracts were with the same landlord for the same subject matter, though for much smaller sums, which is the company's best argument that the manifestation covered small supply deals and not a three-year lease. Even if apparent authority were doubtful, ratification saves the landlord: Ana learned of the lease within a week, knew its material terms, and let the company occupy the kitchen for four months. Acceptance of the benefits with knowledge is ratification by acquiescence, it relates back to February, and it cannot be undone when a cheaper space appears. Estoppel is the fallback: the company's silence plus the landlord's reliance in reserving the space. Bluebonnet is bound. Rico, having exceeded actual authority, owes the company indemnity for any loss, which is why Ana's real remedy is against Rico, not the commissary.

Question A: the cyclist. Respondeat superior asks two things. First, was Rico an employee? Yes: hired to run operations, paid by the company, subject to its control over time, manner and method, driving its truck. Second, was he within the scope of employment when he hit the cyclist? He was on a work trip, but at the moment of the accident he had left the route by fifteen miles for a purely personal errand. A small deviation (detour) stays within scope; a substantial personal departure (frolic) does not, and the employer's liability resumes only when the employee returns to the business route. Fifteen miles for dry cleaning, with the accident in the cleaner's parking lot, looks like a frolic, so Bluebonnet has a solid defense; the cyclist will argue the deviation was brief and the trip was still aimed at the event, and the question of degree goes to the jury. Rico is personally liable in any event, because the tortfeasor always answers for his own negligence. The cyclist may add a direct claim against the company for negligent entrustment or negligent supervision if there are facts about Rico's driving record, and that claim does not depend on scope. Who sues whom: the commissary sues Bluebonnet on the lease; the cyclist sues Rico and Bluebonnet; Bluebonnet cross-claims against Rico for indemnity on both.

Question B: piercing. Start from limited liability: Ana risks only her investment unless an exception applies, and she gave no guaranty and committed no tort herself. The two creditors stand differently. The cyclist is a tort creditor who never chose to deal with a thin LLC; the commissary is a contract creditor who could have asked for a guaranty, and in Texas an owner is liable for a contractual obligation of the entity only if the creditor proves actual fraud, committed primarily for the owner's direct personal benefit (TBOC § 21.223(b), extended to LLC members by § 101.002). For the cyclist, the common-law factors govern: commingling (rent paid from company funds, $38,000 outstanding), assets held in the owner's name (the trucks), capital of $5,000 and no insurance for a business that puts four trucks on the road, and an owner who tells people the entity is paperwork. Minton is the closest case: trivial capital and no insurance for a risky operation, with an owner who runs everything, supports piercing. The cyclist's chances are good, though the court will also ask whether the thin capitalization caused the injury or merely leaves it unpaid. For the commissary, the same facts show domination but not necessarily actual fraud: Ana did not lie to induce the lease, and the paperwork remark, if anything, invited the landlord to rely on her, which supports estoppel-type arguments more than fraud. If Ana drained the account after the lease dispute began, Bridas-style stripping of assets to defeat a creditor would supply the fraud. Gardemal reminds the commissary that control and informality alone are not enough. Prediction: the cyclist likely reaches Ana; the commissary probably does not unless it proves post-dispute asset stripping. The fact that would flip the cyclist's case is insurance: a paid-up liability policy of ordinary size would have answered the undercapitalization argument almost by itself. Advice: separate accounts and no personal expenses, trucks titled to the company, capital and insurance proportionate to the risk, contracts signed in the company's name, and never telling a counterparty that the entity does not matter.

Question C: the owners. Default rules give Priya very little: in an LLC, distributions are made when and as the managers decide, and Ana's salary is a management decision unless it can be shown to breach the duties the company agreement and the statute impose on a manager (§ 101.401 lets the agreement expand or restrict those duties, but it is silent here). Priya may argue that a $150,000 salary with zero distributions for two years is a disguised distribution to one member in breach of the manager's duty of loyalty and good faith; she cannot bring a common-law oppression claim, because Ritchie v. Rupe rejected that cause of action and left only the narrow receivership statute, and she cannot force a buyout. Tomás can sell his economic interest, but under the default rule the buyer becomes an assignee entitled to distributions, not a member, unless the other members consent (§§ 101.108–101.109); the competitor would get Tomás's share of profits without a vote, which makes the offer worth less than it looks. What the agreement should have said: a distribution formula or a cap on member salaries protected by a unanimity requirement to amend; a right of first refusal and a legend so a buyer takes subject to it; buy-sell triggers (death, disability, divorce, departure, deadlock) with a price method (appraisal or capitalized earnings) and funding (key-person insurance, installment notes); a list of decisions needing unanimity, including salaries and new owners; and a deadlock breaker for the day Ana and Tomás disagree. Entity choice: Priya, a nonresident alien, could not own an S corporation, so a corporation would have meant entity-level tax; the LLC taxed as a partnership gives her pass-through treatment and lets the agreement tailor her distribution rights, which is exactly why those rights needed to be written down.

## Common mistakes
- Deriving apparent authority from Rico's title or from what Rico said about himself; only Ana's conduct toward the landlord counts.
- Treating the lapsed insurance as decisive for the contract creditor; the commissary must prove actual fraud for Ana's personal benefit.
- Giving Priya an oppression buyout that Texas abolished in Ritchie v. Rupe.
- Letting the competitor become a member just by buying Tomás's interest.
