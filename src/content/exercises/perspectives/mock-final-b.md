## Facts
Comprehensive mock final (100 points, 120 minutes, in class). Answer all ten questions, about 120 words each: rule, application, authority, result.

1. A memo to a Texas state trial judge argues that a landlord owed no duty to a guest, citing a Restatement section, a Fifth Circuit decision applying Texas law, and a Texas Supreme Court case from 1998. A 2019 Texas statute also addresses landlord duties. Rank these authorities for the judge and say where she should start.

2. Carmen, a Mexican citizen who lives in Monterrey, was injured by a product made by a Texas corporation and wants to sue for $90,000. May she file in federal court in Texas? Which law will decide whether the product was defective? Could she have sued in state court instead?

3. Texas elects its judges. A candidate for a court of appeals tells a civic club that she believes damages caps in medical cases are unconstitutional. The judicial conduct commission sanctions her under a rule forbidding candidates to announce views on disputed legal issues. Analyze her First Amendment claim and explain how the problem would not arise in the federal system.

4. Two plaintiffs sue in federal court. Ana, a taxpayer, challenges a federal statute she considers wasteful; she has no other connection to it. Luis challenges a city rule that barred him from a permit, but the city repealed the rule and issued the permit before the hearing. Can either case proceed?

5. Congress passes a statute allowing either house, by simple resolution, to cancel any regulation issued by the Department of Labor. The House cancels a rule on overtime pay. An employer relies on the cancellation; an employee sues for overtime. Is the cancellation valid?

6. A federal food-labeling statute sets a national format for nutrition labels and states that no state may impose any labeling requirement that is not identical to the federal one. Texas requires an additional front-of-package sugar warning, citing public health. A manufacturer challenges the Texas law. Result?

7. Congress makes it a federal crime to carry a knife with a blade over three inches within 500 feet of any school. The statute contains no findings and no requirement that the knife have traveled in interstate commerce. A defendant arrested outside a Dallas school moves to dismiss. Analyze under the Commerce Clause.

8. A public university creates a scholarship open only to women entering engineering, citing their underrepresentation in the field. A male applicant sues under the Equal Protection Clause. What scrutiny applies and what must the university show?

9. A city ordinance bans all political signs in residential front yards but allows real-estate and other commercial signs of the same size. A homeowner fined for a campaign sign sues. Analyze, and say how the city could regulate yard signs constitutionally.

10. During a traffic stop for a broken taillight, an officer smells marijuana, searches the trunk without a warrant and finds a stolen laptop. The indigent defendant, facing up to two years in prison, is denied appointed counsel. At trial the prosecutor tells the jury that an innocent man would have testified. Identify every constitutional problem.

## Call of the question
Answer all ten questions in order. For each: rule, application, authority, result. Ten points each; partial credit for a correct rule with a weak application.

## Model answer
### Question 1
Start with the 2019 statute: if a statute governs, its text comes first and cases serve to interpret it. The 1998 Texas Supreme Court decision binds the judge on any point the statute did not change, because it is the highest court of her jurisdiction; she must ask whether the statute displaced its rule. The Fifth Circuit decision is a federal court's prediction of Texas law, persuasive only, and weaker if it predates the statute. The Restatement is secondary authority: it explains and persuades but never binds, however often Texas courts have cited it, and it cannot override either the statute or the 1998 case. Ranking for the judge: statute, Texas Supreme Court, then the Fifth Circuit and the Restatement as persuasion.

### Question 2
Federal courts have jurisdiction over suits between a citizen of a state and a citizen of a foreign country when the amount in controversy exceeds $75,000 (28 U.S.C. § 1332(a)(2)). Carmen is a Mexican citizen domiciled abroad, the defendant is a Texas corporation, and $90,000 exceeds the threshold, so the door is open. The claim arises under state law, so even in federal court the substantive rule of decision, here Texas products-liability law, is state law; the federal court applies its own procedure. Carmen could also have sued in a Texas state court, which has general jurisdiction over such claims; the defendant could then remove to federal court because the diversity requirements are met.

### Question 3
A rule forbidding judicial candidates from announcing their views on disputed legal issues is a content-based restriction on core political speech and must survive strict scrutiny: a compelling interest served by narrowly tailored means. Republican Party of Minnesota v. White held that the interest in impartiality means lack of bias toward parties, which the announce clause does not serve, and that impartiality in the sense of having no views is neither possible nor desirable; the clause was not narrowly tailored and fell. The candidate's statement about damages caps is a view on an issue, not a promise about a case or a party, so the sanction violates the First Amendment. The problem is a product of judicial elections; federal judges are nominated by the President and confirmed by the Senate with life tenure, so they never campaign and the tension between campaign speech and impartiality does not arise.

### Question 4
Article III limits federal courts to cases and controversies, which requires standing (a concrete and particularized injury, caused by the defendant and redressable by the court), ripeness and the absence of mootness, and it forbids advisory opinions. Ana's objection to wasteful spending is a generalized grievance shared with every taxpayer; she has no particularized injury, so she lacks standing and her case is dismissed. Luis had standing when he filed, but the repeal and the permit gave him everything he sought; the case is moot unless an exception applies, such as a dispute capable of repetition yet evading review or a voluntary cessation the city could reverse at will. Absent such facts, his case is also dismissed. Neither plaintiff gets an opinion on the merits.

### Question 5
Legislation requires bicameralism and presentment: passage by both houses and presentation to the President for signature or veto (art. I § 7). A one-house resolution that cancels a regulation changes the legal rights of employers and employees outside the legislative process, which is legislative action without the constitutional procedure; the Supreme Court struck the legislative veto on this reasoning in INS v. Chadha. Congress may control agencies by passing a statute that both houses approve and the President signs, by refusing funds, or by narrowing the delegation, but not by a resolution of one chamber. The cancellation is void, the overtime rule remains in force, and the employee's claim proceeds; the employer's reliance on an invalid cancellation is no defense.

### Question 6
Valid federal law displaces conflicting state law, and when Congress says expressly that states may not impose non-identical requirements, the analysis is express preemption: the only question is whether the state rule falls within the clause. A front-of-package sugar warning is a labeling requirement not identical to the federal format, so it is preempted. The state's public-health motive does not matter; the police power is broad, but under the Supremacy Clause it yields where Congress has validly spoken, and labeling of goods sold in interstate commerce is within the commerce power. Texas may lobby for a federal change or regulate in areas the clause leaves open, such as advertising not on the package. The manufacturer wins.

### Question 7
Congress may regulate the channels and instrumentalities of interstate commerce and activities that substantially affect it. Carrying a knife near a school is not economic activity; the statute has no jurisdictional element tying the knife to interstate commerce and no findings; and Lopez rejected aggregation of noneconomic conduct through chains of inference about crime, education and productivity, since that reasoning would leave no activity beyond federal reach. The statute is indistinguishable from the gun-free school zones law Lopez struck down. The motion to dismiss should be granted; Texas may punish the conduct under its police power, and Congress could reach it only by requiring proof that the knife traveled in interstate commerce.

### Question 8
A scholarship limited to women is a classification by sex, which receives intermediate scrutiny: the university must show an important governmental interest and means substantially related to it, with an exceedingly persuasive justification. Remedying underrepresentation in engineering can be an important interest if supported by evidence of past discrimination or present disparities, not by generalizations about what women or men prefer. A benign or remedial purpose does not lower the tier; the court still asks whether the sex-based means fit the interest and whether sex-neutral alternatives, such as outreach or need-based aid, would serve as well. State action is present because the university is public. Result: the scholarship survives only if the university proves the disparity and the fit; the plaintiff has a real claim if the program rests on stereotypes or lacks evidence.

### Question 9
The ordinance regulates speech by its content: it permits commercial signs and bans political ones, and it singles out core political speech at the place where it is cheapest and most personal, the home. Content-based restrictions receive strict scrutiny, and aesthetics and traffic safety are not compelling interests; nor is the ban narrowly tailored when commercial signs of the same size are allowed. The homeowner wins and the fine is void. The city could adopt a content-neutral time, place and manner rule applied to all signs alike, such as limits on size, number, lighting or placement near intersections, narrowly tailored to a significant interest and leaving open ample alternatives; what it cannot do is choose which messages residents may post.

### Question 10
Three problems. First, the search: the automobile exception allows a warrantless search of a vehicle with probable cause, and the smell of marijuana has traditionally supplied it, so the trunk search is likely valid if the officer's observation is credible; if probable cause fails, the laptop is excluded unless an exception such as inevitable discovery applies. Second, counsel: Gideon requires appointed counsel for an indigent defendant in any prosecution that may result in imprisonment, and two years plainly qualifies; the denial is a structural error requiring reversal. Third, the argument: the prosecution bears the burden of proving every element beyond a reasonable doubt, and the Fifth Amendment forbids using the defendant's silence against him; telling the jury that an innocent man would testify is a comment on silence that violates the privilege and requires a new trial.

## Common mistakes
- Citing the Restatement as if a court had to follow it.
- Letting a benign purpose turn intermediate scrutiny into rational basis for the scholarship.
- Treating the yard-sign ordinance as content neutral because it is about signs; the line it draws is political versus commercial.
- Forgetting that the smell of marijuana supports probable cause, so the search question is closer than the counsel and silence questions.
