## Facts
Comprehensive mock final (100 points, 120 minutes, in class, closed notes unless the professor says otherwise). Answer all ten questions. Each is worth ten points and deserves about 120 words: state the rule, apply it to the facts, name the case or clause, and say who wins.

1. A federal statute directs the Department of Transportation to set weight limits for trucks on interstate highways, and the Department issues a regulation setting 80,000 pounds. Texas enacts a statute allowing 90,000 pounds; the City of Dallas passes an ordinance capping trucks at 70,000 pounds on all roads within the city, including interstates. A trucker is cited under both. Which rule governs on an interstate highway in Dallas, and why?

2. A trial judge in Fort Worth must decide whether a text message can satisfy a Texas statute's writing requirement. The Austin court of appeals said yes in 2023; a Fifth Circuit panel applying Texas law said no in 2024; the Texas Supreme Court has never ruled, though in a 2021 opinion on a different issue it remarked that electronic messages are writings. What binds the Fort Worth judge, what persuades her, and how should she treat the 2021 remark?

3. Congress enacts a statute giving the Supreme Court original jurisdiction over every suit challenging a federal agency's rule, so that challengers may file directly in the Court. A company files its petition there. What does the Court do, and what questions does it ask to get there?

4. Dockworkers strike at the ports of Houston and Corpus Christi. The President orders the Secretary of Transportation to take possession of the private terminals and run them with federal employees, citing the national economy. Two years earlier Congress had considered and voted down a bill that would have authorized such seizures during labor disputes. The terminal owners sue. Analyze.

5. Congress enacts a statute requiring every private day-care center that uses toys, furniture or food purchased from out of state to meet federal safety standards, and provides for fines. A Lubbock day-care center that serves only local families challenges the statute as beyond the commerce power. Who wins?

6. A new federal firearms statute requires county sheriffs to run background checks and maintain a federal registry, and a separate provision cuts five percent of federal highway funds to any state whose sheriffs refuse. Texas challenges both provisions. Analyze each.

7. Two children are born in the same Houston hospital on the same day: one to a Mexican couple visiting on tourist visas, the other to an accredited foreign diplomat posted to the consulate. Is either child a citizen of the United States? Explain the text and the reading in Elk v. Wilkins; if you know of current litigation about the clause, say what is at stake without predicting it.

8. A state statute directs adoption agencies to place children only with adoptive parents of the same race as the child, citing the child's need for cultural continuity. A couple denied a placement sues. Which constitutional provisions apply, what level of scrutiny, and what result?

9. A city ordinance caps food-delivery couriers at ten hours of driving per day and requires platforms to pay a minimum fee per delivery. A platform sues, arguing that the ordinance violates its liberty of contract under the Due Process Clause. How would the court analyze the claim in 1905 and how does it analyze it today, and why did the change happen?

10. Police arrest Diego, seventeen, for a robbery in which a store clerk was killed by Diego's accomplice. At the station, without warnings, officers question him for three hours and he confesses. At trial the judge denies his request for a jury because the robbery count carries a maximum of eighteen months, and the prosecutor seeks the death penalty on the murder count. Identify every constitutional problem.

## Call of the question
Answer all ten questions in order. For each: rule, application, authority, result. Ten points each; partial credit for a correct rule with a weak application.

## Model answer
### Question 1
Under the Supremacy Clause, valid federal law prevails over conflicting state and local law, and a regulation issued within the authority Congress delegated has the force of a statute. The federal 80,000-pound limit therefore displaces the Texas 90,000-pound statute on interstate highways, where the two conflict (conflict preemption: a trucker cannot comply with both if Texas purports to authorize what federal law forbids, and the state rule obstructs the federal scheme). The Dallas ordinance loses twice: a city has only the powers the state gives it, so a Texas statute controls an inconsistent ordinance, and the federal limit occupies the field on interstates. Result: 80,000 pounds governs; the state citation fails to the extent it relies on the 90,000 limit, and the city citation fails on an interstate.

### Question 2
Only decisions of higher courts in the same jurisdiction bind. The Texas Supreme Court has not decided the question, so nothing binds the Fort Worth judge; the Austin court of appeals is a sister intermediate court, persuasive but not binding, and a Fifth Circuit reading of Texas law is a federal court's prediction of what Texas would do, also persuasive only. The 2021 remark is dictum: it was not necessary to the decision of a different issue, so it does not bind, though as a signal from the court that will eventually decide the matter it carries weight. The judge should decide on the statute's text first, then weigh the reasoning of the two opinions, giving the Austin decision special attention because the Texas Supreme Court may later resolve the split.

### Question 3
Marbury asks three questions: does the plaintiff have a right, does the law give a remedy, and can this court grant it? The third question answers the problem. Article III lists the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction (cases affecting ambassadors and those in which a state is a party) and gives it appellate jurisdiction over the rest; Marbury held that Congress cannot add to the original list by statute. A statute sending agency challenges straight to the Court is repugnant to Article III, and because the Constitution is paramount law, the Court must apply it over the statute and dismiss the petition for want of jurisdiction. That act of refusing to enforce an unconstitutional statute in the case before it is judicial review. The company must start in a district court or a court of appeals as the governing statutes provide.

### Question 4
Presidential action must rest on the Constitution or on an act of Congress; there is no inherent power to seize private property in a domestic emergency. Under Jackson's framework, the President's power is greatest when Congress authorizes him, uncertain when Congress is silent, and at its lowest ebb when he acts against Congress's expressed or implied will. Congress considered seizure authority for labor disputes and voted it down, so the order sits in the third zone, exactly where the steel seizure sat in 1952. The commander-in-chief and take-care powers do not reach private terminals at home; the economy is a reason for Congress to legislate, not a source of presidential lawmaking. The owners win, and the court orders the terminals returned.

### Question 5
Congress may regulate the channels and instrumentalities of interstate commerce and activities that substantially affect it; for economic activity, the effects of many small actors may be aggregated, and the Court asks only whether Congress had a rational basis for finding the effect (Heart of Atlanta, where a motel's service to interstate travelers sufficed). Running a day-care center for pay is economic activity; the toys, furniture and food crossed state lines, which gives the statute a jurisdictional hook; and the safety of children in thousands of such centers rationally affects the market for those goods and the labor market of working parents. Lopez and Morrison limit Congress only for noneconomic activity with no jurisdictional element, which this statute is not. The Lubbock center loses.

### Question 6
The Tenth Amendment reserves to the states the powers not delegated, and the anti-commandeering rule forbids Congress from ordering state legislatures to enact laws or state officers to administer a federal program. Requiring sheriffs to run federal background checks and keep a federal registry commandeers state officers; that provision falls, whatever the commerce power might otherwise support. Conditional spending is different: Congress may attach conditions to federal funds if the condition is clear, related to the purpose of the funds, not itself unconstitutional, and not so large that it coerces rather than persuades. A five percent cut of highway funds tied to firearms enforcement is modest in amount but weakly related to highways; the state has a real argument on relatedness, though courts have upheld similar cuts. Likely result: the mandate fails, the funding condition is a closer question and probably survives.

### Question 7
The Fourteenth Amendment makes a citizen of every person born in the United States and subject to the jurisdiction thereof. Elk v. Wilkins read the second phrase to require birth completely subject to the political jurisdiction of the United States, and it listed children of foreign diplomats, like members of tribes at the time, as persons not so subject: diplomats enjoy immunity and owe allegiance elsewhere. The diplomat's child is therefore not a citizen by birth and would need naturalization. The tourists are fully subject to American law while here, and the long-standing understanding has been that their child is a citizen at birth. Chae Chan Ping adds that Congress controls admission and exclusion of foreigners, but admission is a different question from citizenship by birth. The scope of the phrase for children of noncitizens is the subject of current litigation; what is at stake is whether the Elk reading of jurisdiction extends beyond diplomats and similar cases, a question of text and history for the courts, not for an answer sheet.

### Question 8
The statute classifies by race, so the Equal Protection Clause applies and the classification gets strict scrutiny: the state must show a compelling interest and a law narrowly tailored to it. Loving rejected the argument that a racial rule applied equally to all races escapes scrutiny, and it treated racial classifications as justified, if ever, only by something far weightier than tradition or presumed social benefit. Cultural continuity may be a legitimate consideration in an individual placement, but a flat statutory bar is not narrowly tailored and treats race as decisive, the approach Brown condemned. The family interest also implicates due process, since forming a family is a protected liberty. State action is present because the statute binds agencies. Result: the statute is unconstitutional and the couple wins.

### Question 9
In 1905 the Court in Lochner struck a maximum-hours law for bakers because the Due Process Clause protected liberty of contract and the state had not shown a genuine health or safety justification; it would have read hour caps and fee floors for couriers the same way. Since 1937 the Court has abandoned that approach: economic regulation is reviewed for rational basis only, and a court asks whether any legitimate purpose, such as road safety or fair pay, could rationally support the ordinance. Footnote four of Carolene Products explains the change: close review is reserved for laws that restrict the political process, invade specific constitutional rights, or burden discrete and insular minorities; ordinary economic legislation is left to the legislature. The platform loses. This is the line between economic liberalism, which Lochner protected, and the civil-rights liberalism of Brown and Loving.

### Question 10
Three problems. First, the confession: Diego was in custody and interrogated without being told of his rights to silence and counsel, so under Miranda the statement is inadmissible in the prosecution's case, and three hours of questioning of a seventeen-year-old aggravates the voluntariness concern. Second, the jury: the Sixth Amendment, applied to the states through the Fourteenth in Duncan, guarantees a jury for serious offenses, and an offense punishable by more than six months is serious; eighteen months qualifies, so the denial is reversible error, and the murder count independently requires a jury. Third, the sentence: Roper holds that the Eighth and Fourteenth Amendments forbid the death penalty for a crime committed before age eighteen, under evolving standards of decency and the reduced culpability of juveniles; the prosecutor's request is unconstitutional on its face. Diego remains triable on both counts with a jury, without the confession, and without the death penalty.

## Common mistakes
- Treating a federal regulation as weaker than a state statute; if authorized, it carries the Supremacy Clause.
- Calling a sister court of appeals or the Fifth Circuit binding on a Texas trial court.
- Placing the port seizure in Jackson's twilight zone; Congress spoke by rejecting the bill.
- Forgetting that Duncan measures seriousness by the authorized penalty, not the sentence imposed.
