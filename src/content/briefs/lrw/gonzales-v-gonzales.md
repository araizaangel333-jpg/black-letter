## Facts
The parties married in February 2019 and separated in March 2020. In the divorce the wife pleaded assault and intentional infliction of emotional distress. She described being pushed against a nightstand (with photographs of bruises) and pinned against a wall with the child's car seat; the husband admitted threatening to kill her. Over the marriage he threatened to fight her to the death and to take the child, broke her laptop in rages, called her names, installed hidden cameras in the home that he kept accessing after separation, sat outside her house at night, overdrew the joint account and withheld community funds, locked the pantry, and caused the child's removal from daycare. Her therapist diagnosed an anxiety disorder and treated panic attacks and depression; friends and a coworker described the change in her.

## Procedural posture
After a bench trial the court granted the divorce, awarded $132,000 on the tort claims (findings later characterized the award as compensatory and exemplary damages), enjoined the husband from threatening, harassing or recording her, divided the estate disproportionately in her favor, ordered retroactive child support plus a separate money judgment, and awarded attorney's fees. The husband appealed. The court of appeals affirmed everything except the separate money judgment for retroactive support, which it reversed.

## Issue
Whether legally and factually sufficient evidence supported each element of IIED and the exemplary damages, and whether the decree's other provisions were within the trial court's discretion.

## Holding
Yes as to IIED and exemplary damages. The evidence of the course of conduct supported intent or recklessness, extreme and outrageous conduct, causation and severe distress; the assaultive conduct supported malice for exemplary damages. The money judgment for retroactive support was reversed because the Family Code did not authorize it in these circumstances.

## Reasoning
The court applied the four-element test (citing the Supreme Court of Texas's modern restatement of it) and the standards for reviewing findings after a bench trial, presuming omitted findings that the evidence supports. On intent, it followed Twyman's recklessness definition and Standard Fruit's primary-risk requirement and allowed inference from conduct. On outrageousness, it evaluated the whole course of conduct, as GTE Southwest requires, and rejected the idea that a troubled marriage lowers the standard. On severity, it held that the plaintiff need not be unable to perform daily tasks: treatment, diagnosis and corroboration were enough, and the timing of her therapy supported causation. It also enforced Twyman's rule that the same conduct cannot be counted twice, once as tort damages and again in the property division, and found independent grounds for the disproportionate division.

## Rule (own words)
Prove IIED inside a marriage with a pattern, not an incident; prove severity with a professional's diagnosis and other people's observations, not only the plaintiff's word; and keep the tort recovery separate from the property split.

## What-ifs
- One slap and a week of insults: outrageousness fails under the ordinary-marital-dispute line.
- No therapist, only the plaintiff's testimony of sleeplessness: severity becomes the weak element.
- The same threats pleaded only as a basis for a disproportionate division: no tort damages, but the division may still stand.
