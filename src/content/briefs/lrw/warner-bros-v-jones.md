## Facts
An online tabloid published a story reporting that a former Dallas Cowboys player had been accused of trying to hire someone to kill a relative. The player's lawyer issued a press release the same day demanding a retraction; the outlet updated the story. The player sued the outlet, its corporate parents and the reporter for defamation, conspiracy, intentional infliction of emotional distress, malicious prosecution and abuse of process.

## Procedural posture
The defendants moved to dismiss under the Texas Citizens Participation Act, which requires the plaintiff to show a prima facie case for each element by clear and specific evidence once the statute applies. The trial court denied the motion. The court of appeals affirmed as to defamation and conspiracy and reversed as to IIED, malicious prosecution and abuse of process. The Supreme Court of Texas granted review on the defamation issues and affirmed in May 2020.

## Issue
For this course: whether an IIED claim can proceed when the alleged injury comes from a published story that is also the basis of a defamation claim.

## Holding
No. IIED is a gap-filler; the gravamen was defamation, so the IIED claim was dismissed under the anti-SLAPP statute. (Verify the court's exact language and the pincite in the 2017 opinion.)

## Reasoning
The court applied Zeltwanger's rule that IIED exists only where no other theory of recovery addresses the harm, and treated the publication-based injury as defamation territory, the same move as Draker for a fake online profile.

## Rule (own words)
A story that hurts your reputation is a defamation case. You do not get a second tort for the distress the story caused.
