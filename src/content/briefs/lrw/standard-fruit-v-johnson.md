## Facts
A Standard Fruit delivery driver drove recklessly and caused a collision. A person affected by the crash sued the company for intentional infliction of emotional distress based on the driver's conduct and the distress of the incident.

## Procedural posture
The plaintiff obtained a judgment on the IIED claim; the court of appeals affirmed. The Supreme Court of Texas held the evidence legally insufficient on the intent element and rendered a take-nothing judgment on that claim.

## Issue
Whether reckless driving that causes an accident can satisfy the "intentional or reckless" element of IIED when the distress was not the object or primary risk of the conduct.

## Holding
No. The driver's conduct risked physical harm; any emotional distress was incidental, so the first element was not met.

## Reasoning
The tort is intentional: the defendant must direct the conduct at causing severe emotional distress, know it is substantially certain to result, or consciously disregard a high probability of it. Reckless driving creates a risk of injury, not a design to distress, and allowing IIED on such facts would convert ordinary accident cases into intentional torts.

## Rule (own words)
Intent and recklessness are measured against the distress itself. If the defendant was aiming at something else, element one fails.

## What-ifs
- If the driver had deliberately swerved at the plaintiff to frighten him, element one would be met.
- The analysis pairs with element two: conduct that is intended to distress still has to be outrageous.
