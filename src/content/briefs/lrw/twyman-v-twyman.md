## Facts
During a divorce, the wife sought damages for emotional distress, alleging that her husband had pressured her into sexual practices that he knew caused her anguish because of a past trauma. The trial court awarded damages on a negligent infliction theory.

## Procedural posture
The court of appeals affirmed. While the appeal was pending, the Supreme Court of Texas rejected negligent infliction of emotional distress as an independent tort (Boyles v. Kerr). In Twyman the court recognized intentional infliction instead, reversed and remanded for a new trial.

## Issue
Whether Texas recognizes intentional infliction of emotional distress as an independent tort, and whether it may be asserted in a divorce proceeding.

## Holding
Yes to both. The court adopted Restatement (Second) of Torts § 46 and its four elements, and allowed the claim in a divorce subject to a bar on double recovery.

## Reasoning
Most states had adopted § 46; the tort fills a gap for intentional conduct so outrageous that no reasonable person should have to endure the resulting distress. Recklessness suffices for the first element. Because the trial court used the wrong theory, the facts had to be re-evaluated under the new elements.

## Rule (own words)
Four elements, all required: intent or recklessness; extreme and outrageous conduct; causation; severe distress. The tort is narrow by design.

## What-ifs
- If the conduct is merely insulting or inconsiderate, element two fails (GTE Southwest).
- If another tort covers the harm, IIED is unavailable as a gap-filler (Hoffmann-La Roche v. Zeltwanger).
