## Facts
Mildred Jeter, a Black and Native American woman, and Richard Loving, a white man, married in Washington, D.C. in 1958 and returned to live in Virginia. Virginia's Racial Integrity Act made their marriage a felony. They were indicted, pleaded guilty and were sentenced to a year in jail, suspended on condition that they leave Virginia and not return together for 25 years.

## Procedural posture
Years later the Lovings moved to vacate the judgment as unconstitutional. The state trial court denied the motion; the Virginia Supreme Court of Appeals upheld the statutes. The U.S. Supreme Court granted review and reversed.

## Issue
Whether a state statute that prevents marriages between persons solely on the basis of race violates the Equal Protection and Due Process Clauses of the Fourteenth Amendment.

## Holding
Yes, on both grounds. The convictions were reversed.

## Reasoning
Equal protection: the statutes rest solely on race; such classifications are "odious to a free people" and must survive the most rigid scrutiny; there is no legitimate purpose independent of invidious discrimination, and the law's history shows it was designed to preserve white supremacy. The state's "equal application" argument fails because the Fourteenth Amendment's purpose was to eliminate official sources of racial discrimination, not to tolerate symmetrical ones. Due process: the freedom to marry is one of the vital personal rights essential to the orderly pursuit of happiness; denying it on racial grounds deprives citizens of liberty without due process.

## Rule (own words)
Race-based restrictions on marriage are unconstitutional twice over: as a racial classification without legitimate purpose and as an intrusion on a fundamental right.

## What-ifs
- Replace race with sex: the Court in Obergefell (2015) used the same due process and equal protection pairing.
- If the state offered a "legitimate" purpose, strict scrutiny would still require narrow tailoring, which a flat ban cannot meet.
