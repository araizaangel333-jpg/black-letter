## Facts
Gary Duncan, a nineteen-year-old Black man in Plaquemines Parish, Louisiana, stopped his car when he saw his younger cousins, who had recently integrated a white school, in a confrontation with white boys on the roadside. He encouraged his cousins to leave and, according to the white boys, slapped one of them on the arm. He was charged with simple battery, a misdemeanor punishable by up to two years in prison and a $300 fine. Louisiana allowed jury trials only where capital punishment or hard labor could be imposed, so his request for a jury was denied. The judge convicted him and sentenced him to sixty days and a $150 fine.

## Procedural posture
The Louisiana Supreme Court declined to review. The U.S. Supreme Court noted probable jurisdiction and reversed.

## Issue
Whether the Fourteenth Amendment guarantees a right to jury trial in state prosecutions where a sentence as long as two years may be imposed.

## Holding
Yes. The Sixth Amendment jury right is fundamental and binds the states; an offense carrying a possible two-year sentence is serious, so Duncan was entitled to a jury.

## Reasoning
The Court traced the jury from Magna Carta through the colonial grievances, the state constitutions and the Sixth Amendment, concluding that the jury is a safeguard against corrupt or overzealous prosecutors and compliant, biased or eccentric judges, and that every state already provided it in serious cases. Under the selective incorporation approach the question is whether the right is fundamental to the American scheme of justice, and it is. Justice Harlan's dissent, joined by Stewart, defended the older view that the Fourteenth Amendment requires fundamental fairness, not the specific federal procedures, and warned that incorporation freezes state experimentation.

## Rule (own words)
Serious crime, serious punishment possible, jury required, in any American court. The states cannot route their criminal justice around the jury.

## What-ifs
- A traffic offense punishable by a $200 fine: petty, no jury.
- A state that allows 11–1 verdicts: unconstitutional after Ramos.
- A civil case in state court: the Seventh Amendment civil jury has never been incorporated, so the state decides.
