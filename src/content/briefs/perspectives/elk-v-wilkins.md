## Facts
John Elk was born a member of an Indian tribe, later severed his tribal relations and lived in Omaha, Nebraska, among non-Native citizens. When he tried to register to vote, the registrar, Wilkins, refused on the ground that Elk was not a citizen.

## Procedural posture
Elk sued under the Civil Rights Act for the refusal to register him, claiming citizenship under the Fourteenth Amendment. The circuit court sustained a demurrer. The Supreme Court affirmed.

## Issue
Whether a Native American born a tribal member, who later leaves the tribe, is a citizen of the United States under the Citizenship Clause.

## Holding
No. He was not "subject to the jurisdiction" of the United States at birth, and no later act of naturalization made him a citizen.

## Reasoning
The Court read "subject to the jurisdiction thereof" to mean completely subject to the political jurisdiction and owing direct allegiance, not merely present within the territory. Tribes were treated as distinct political communities, so their members stood like children of foreign ministers. Naturalization required an affirmative act by the United States (treaty or statute), and an individual could not naturalize himself by leaving the tribe.

## Rule (own words)
Birth within the territory was not enough in 1884; the Court demanded birth within the nation's political jurisdiction. The decision left tribal members outside citizenship until Congress acted in 1924.

## What-ifs
- Compare United States v. Wong Kim Ark (1898): a child born in San Francisco to Chinese parents was a citizen, because his parents were subject to U.S. jurisdiction, unlike tribal members.
- The case is the historical background for today's debates about the meaning of "subject to the jurisdiction."
