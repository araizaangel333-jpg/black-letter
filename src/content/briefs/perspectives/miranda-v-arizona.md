## Facts
Ernesto Miranda was arrested at his home in Phoenix in 1963, taken to the police station, identified by the complaining witness, and questioned for two hours by two officers without being told he could remain silent or have a lawyer. He signed a confession to kidnapping and rape on a form whose printed paragraph recited that the statement was voluntary and made with full knowledge of his legal rights. The confession was admitted at trial and he was convicted. The three companion cases involved similar station-house interrogations in New York, by the FBI in Kansas City, and in Los Angeles.

## Procedural posture
The Arizona Supreme Court affirmed Miranda's conviction, holding that his rights were not violated because he had not asked for counsel. The U.S. Supreme Court granted certiorari in the four cases to decide what safeguards the Fifth Amendment requires in custodial interrogation, and reversed.

## Issue
Whether statements obtained from a defendant questioned while in custody are admissible when the police gave no warnings of the privilege against self-incrimination and the right to counsel.

## Holding
No. Absent other fully effective safeguards, the prosecution may not use statements stemming from custodial interrogation unless the suspect was warned of his rights, was given the opportunity to exercise them, and voluntarily, knowingly and intelligently waived them. Miranda's confession was inadmissible; the conviction was reversed.

## Reasoning
The Court reviewed police interrogation manuals and concluded that custodial questioning is designed to overbear the will of the suspect through isolation and psychological pressure. The Fifth Amendment privilege, which the Court had applied to the states two years earlier, protects a person at the station house as much as in the courtroom; the warnings and the right to have counsel present are the means of making that protection effective for people who do not know their rights. The waiver burden is on the state, silence is not a waiver, and any indication that the suspect wants to stop or wants a lawyer ends the questioning. The dissenters (Harlan, White, Clark) objected that the rule would hamper legitimate law enforcement and went beyond the Constitution's text.

## Rule (own words)
Custody plus interrogation triggers the warnings. No warnings, no waiver, no confession in evidence. Invocation stops the questioning.

## What-ifs
- A suspect blurts out a confession in the patrol car before any question: no interrogation, so Miranda does not apply.
- Police ask a handcuffed suspect where the gun is in a crowded store: the public safety exception allows the answer.
- The suspect, warned, says nothing for three hours and then answers a question: Berghuis v. Thompkins (2010) treats the answer as an implied waiver because silence alone is not an invocation.

## Class notes
Miranda, Duncan and Roper were all decided by closely divided Courts; the discussion is as much about the Warren and Kennedy Courts' view of their own role as about the rights.
