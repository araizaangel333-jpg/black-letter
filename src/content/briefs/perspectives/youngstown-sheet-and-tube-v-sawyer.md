## Facts
During the Korean War, facing a nationwide steel strike, President Truman ordered the Secretary of Commerce to seize and operate most of the country's steel mills, citing the war emergency and his powers as President and Commander in Chief. He reported to Congress, which did nothing. No statute authorized the seizure, and the Taft-Hartley Act of 1947 had deliberately omitted seizure as a remedy.

## Procedural posture
The companies obtained an injunction in the district court; the Supreme Court took the case quickly and affirmed.

## Issue
Whether the President had constitutional or statutory authority to seize private steel mills.

## Holding
No. The order was lawmaking, which the Constitution entrusts to Congress alone; neither the executive power clause nor the commander-in-chief clause supplies it.

## Reasoning
Justice Black's majority opinion is formal: the President executes laws, he does not make them. Justice Jackson's concurrence, the part everyone cites, treats presidential power as a sliding scale that depends on what Congress has done: authorization, silence or disapproval. Here Congress had disapproved, so the President stood at his lowest ebb and could rely only on his own powers minus those of Congress.

## Rule (own words)
Ask first what Congress has said. Then ask what the Constitution gives the President alone. A domestic emergency does not create a third source.
