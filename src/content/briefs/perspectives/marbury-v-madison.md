## Facts
In the last days of John Adams's presidency, Congress created new judgeships and Adams appointed Federalists to fill them; the Senate confirmed and Adams signed the commissions, but some, including William Marbury's commission as a justice of the peace for the District of Columbia, were not delivered before Jefferson took office. Jefferson ordered his Secretary of State, James Madison, to withhold them.

## Procedural posture
Marbury and others asked the Supreme Court directly, under section 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789, for a writ of mandamus compelling delivery. The Court heard the case in its original jurisdiction and dismissed for want of jurisdiction.

## Issue
Whether Marbury had a right to the commission, whether the law afforded him a remedy, and whether the Supreme Court could issue that remedy in an original action.

## Holding
Marbury had a vested right to the commission and mandamus was the proper remedy, but the Supreme Court could not issue it: section 13, read to authorize original mandamus against federal officers, conflicted with Article III's definition of the Court's original jurisdiction, and a statute repugnant to the Constitution cannot be applied by the courts.

## Reasoning
The commission was complete when signed and sealed; delivery was a ministerial act. For every legal right there must be a remedy, and courts may order an executive officer to perform a duty the law imposes, as opposed to reviewing discretionary political acts. But Article III gives the Supreme Court original jurisdiction only in cases involving ambassadors and states, and appellate jurisdiction otherwise; Congress cannot add to the original list. Faced with a statute and the Constitution pointing in different directions, a court must prefer the Constitution: it is the supreme, written law, the judicial power extends to cases arising under it, and judges swear to support it. It is the judiciary's province to say what the law is.

## Rule (own words)
Judicial review in one sentence: when a statute and the Constitution conflict in a case, the court applies the Constitution and sets the statute aside for that case. Everything else (precedent, general effects, the Court's prestige) is what turns that case-by-case power into a system.

## What-ifs
- If Marbury had sued in a lower federal court, the jurisdictional problem would not have arisen and the Court might have had to confront Jefferson directly.
- If Congress had instead removed a subject from the Court's appellate jurisdiction, Article III expressly allows exceptions and regulations, a very different question.
- If a state statute conflicts with the federal Constitution, the Supremacy Clause makes the answer even clearer and state judges are bound.
