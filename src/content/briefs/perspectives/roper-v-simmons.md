## Facts
At seventeen, Christopher Simmons planned a burglary and murder with two younger friends, telling them they could get away with it because they were minors. He and one friend broke into a woman's home, bound her with duct tape and electrical wire, drove her to a railroad trestle and threw her into the river, where she drowned. He confessed and reenacted the crime on video. A Missouri jury convicted him of murder and, after the prosecution argued that his age should count against him, recommended death; the trial court imposed it.

## Procedural posture
State and federal postconviction challenges failed. After Atkins v. Virginia (2002) held that executing the intellectually disabled is unconstitutional, Simmons filed a new petition arguing the same reasoning covered juveniles. The Missouri Supreme Court agreed and resentenced him to life without parole; the state sought certiorari and the U.S. Supreme Court affirmed.

## Issue
Whether the Eighth Amendment, applied to the states through the Fourteenth, permits the death penalty for a crime committed when the offender was under eighteen.

## Holding
No. The Court overruled Stanford v. Kentucky (1989), which had allowed execution for crimes committed at sixteen or seventeen, and drew a categorical line at eighteen.

## Reasoning
The Court applied its two-step Eighth Amendment method. First, objective indicia of consensus: the number of states rejecting the practice, the rarity of its use, and the direction of change, all of which mirrored the evidence that had sufficed in Atkins. Second, the Court's independent judgment: juveniles' lack of maturity, susceptibility to pressure and unformed character make them less culpable, so the death penalty's retributive and deterrent justifications weaken, and a categorical rule is safer than case-by-case assessment because juries may be swayed by the brutality of a crime. The Court then noted the overwhelming weight of international opinion against the juvenile death penalty as confirmation of its conclusion. Justice O'Connor dissented on the strength of the consensus and the use of a bright line; Justice Scalia's dissent rejected the counting of states, the Court's claim to independent moral judgment, and the reliance on foreign law.

## Rule (own words)
Eighth Amendment analysis counts the states, watches the trend, and then the Court decides for itself whether the punishment fits the class of offender; foreign practice can confirm but not decide.

## What-ifs
- Life without parole for a juvenile who did not kill: barred by Graham v. Florida (2010).
- Mandatory life without parole for a juvenile murderer: barred by Miller v. Alabama (2012).
- A defendant who was eighteen and one day: the line is categorical, so the death penalty remains available.

## Class notes
Keep the method separate from the result: a student from a country without the death penalty should still be able to explain why the Court counts states rather than simply declaring the punishment immoral.
