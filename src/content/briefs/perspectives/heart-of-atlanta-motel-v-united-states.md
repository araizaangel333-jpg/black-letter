## Facts
The Heart of Atlanta Motel, located near Interstates 75 and 85, advertised nationally and drew most of its guests from other states. It refused to rent rooms to Black customers and intended to continue after the Civil Rights Act of 1964 took effect.

## Procedural posture
The motel sued for a declaration that Title II (public accommodations) was unconstitutional and an injunction against its enforcement; the United States counterclaimed. A three-judge district court upheld the Act and enjoined the motel. The motel appealed directly to the Supreme Court, which affirmed.

## Issue
Whether Congress had power under the Commerce Clause to prohibit racial discrimination in places of public accommodation that serve interstate travelers.

## Holding
Yes. Title II is a valid exercise of the commerce power as applied to the motel.

## Reasoning
Congress heard extensive evidence that discrimination in lodging obstructed interstate travel by Black Americans and burdened commerce. The power to regulate commerce includes the power to regulate local activities that have a substantial and harmful effect on it, and the test is only whether Congress had a rational basis for its finding and chose reasonable means. That the problem was also moral does not remove it from the commerce power. The Fifth Amendment takings and Thirteenth Amendment arguments were rejected.

## Rule (own words)
Local conduct that affects interstate commerce is within Congress's reach, and courts ask only whether Congress acted rationally.

## What-ifs
- Compare United States v. Lopez (1995): guns near schools were not economic activity, so the commerce power did not reach them; the motel's lodging business was.
- If the motel served only locals, the commerce link would be weaker, though Katzenbach v. McClung (restaurant buying food that crossed state lines) shows the link can be indirect.
