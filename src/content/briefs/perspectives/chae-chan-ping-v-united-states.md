## Facts
Chae Chan Ping, a Chinese laborer who had lived in San Francisco since 1875, left for China in 1887 holding a government certificate that entitled him to return under the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 as amended. While he was abroad, Congress passed the Scott Act of 1888, which annulled such certificates. On his return he was refused landing.

## Procedural posture
He petitioned for a writ of habeas corpus in the federal circuit court, which denied it. He appealed to the Supreme Court, which affirmed.

## Issue
Whether Congress could bar his re-entry despite the treaty with China and the certificate issued under the earlier statute.

## Holding
Yes. The exclusion was valid; the certificate conferred no vested right and the later statute displaced the treaty as domestic law.

## Reasoning
Every sovereign nation has the power to exclude foreigners; in the United States that power rests with the political branches and is not subject to judicial control. Treaties and statutes stand on the same footing, so the later enactment governs. The certificate was a license revocable by Congress. Any breach of the treaty was a matter for diplomacy, not the courts.

## Rule (own words)
Immigration control is a sovereign power exercised by Congress with little judicial review, and a later statute overrides an inconsistent earlier treaty.
