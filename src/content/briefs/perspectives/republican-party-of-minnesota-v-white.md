## Facts
Minnesota elects its judges. Its code of judicial conduct contained an announce clause prohibiting candidates from announcing their views on disputed legal or political issues. A lawyer running for the state supreme court wanted to criticize prior decisions and discuss issues; he and the state Republican Party sued.

## Procedural posture
Lower federal courts upheld the clause as serving judicial impartiality; the Supreme Court reversed.

## Issue
Whether the announce clause violates the First Amendment.

## Holding
Yes. The clause restricts core political speech on the basis of content and is not narrowly tailored to a compelling interest: it does not serve impartiality toward parties, it cannot sensibly aim at having judges with no views on the law, and open-mindedness is not served by a rule that only muzzles candidates while sitting judges write opinions on the same issues.

## Reasoning
Strict scrutiny applies; the dissents stressed that judicial elections differ from legislative ones because judges apply law rather than represent constituents. The majority answered that a state that chooses to elect judges cannot then forbid the speech that makes elections meaningful.

## Rule (own words)
Elect judges and you accept campaigns; states may forbid promises about cases, not opinions about the law.
