## Facts
Black children in four states were required by law or by official policy to attend schools separate from white children. Their parents sued, arguing that the segregated schools could never be equal and that the segregation itself violated the Equal Protection Clause. In the Kansas case the lower court found the Black and white schools substantially equal in buildings, curricula and teacher qualifications, and denied relief under Plessy v. Ferguson (1896).

## Procedural posture
Three-judge federal district courts denied relief in Kansas, South Carolina and Virginia; the Delaware courts ordered admission to the white schools because of inequality but without rejecting segregation. The Supreme Court consolidated the cases, ordered reargument on the history of the Fourteenth Amendment, and reversed.

## Issue
Whether segregation of children in public schools solely on the basis of race, even where tangible factors are equal, deprives the minority children of equal educational opportunities under the Equal Protection Clause.

## Holding
Yes. Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal; the plaintiffs were deprived of the equal protection of the laws. The question of remedy was set for further argument (Brown II, 1955).

## Reasoning
The history of the Fourteenth Amendment did not settle the question. The Court instead looked at the place of public education in 1954: the foundation of citizenship, the principal instrument for awakening cultural values and preparing for later life, and something the state must provide on equal terms once it provides it at all. Separating children because of race generates a feeling of inferiority that may affect their hearts and minds in a way unlikely ever to be undone, a finding supported by modern psychological knowledge (the famous footnote 11). Whatever Plessy said about railroad cars, that reasoning has no place in public education.

## Rule (own words)
Equality of buildings and books does not cure a system whose message is inferiority. Where the state classifies by race in public education, the classification itself is the injury.

## What-ifs
- If a school district were racially imbalanced because of housing patterns rather than official policy, Brown would not by itself require a remedy (de jure versus de facto segregation).
- If the state had offered Black students scholarships to out-of-state schools instead of admission, the Court's earlier cases (Gaines, Sweatt) already treated that as unequal.
- Replace race with sex: the Court later applied intermediate, not strict, scrutiny (United States v. Virginia, 1996).

## Class notes
Note 2 in the book is where the discussion lives: the Court announced the right in 1954, prescribed the remedy slowly in 1955, and had to insist in Cooper v. Aaron (1958) that state officials are bound by its interpretation of the Constitution. Ask what Brown would have been without Little Rock, the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and federal funding conditions.
