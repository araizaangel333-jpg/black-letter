## Facts
New York's Bakeshop Act of 1895 forbade bakery employees from working more than ten hours a day or sixty hours a week. Joseph Lochner owned a bakery in Utica and was fined for allowing an employee to work beyond the limit. He argued that the law took away his and his workers' liberty to agree on hours.

## Procedural posture
Convicted in state court and fined; the New York appellate courts affirmed the conviction and upheld the statute. The U.S. Supreme Court reversed, 5–4.

## Issue
Whether a state maximum-hours law for bakers is a legitimate exercise of the police power, or an unreasonable interference with the liberty of contract protected by the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause.

## Holding
The law was an unconstitutional interference with liberty of contract. It was not a genuine health measure: baking was not an unusually unhealthy trade, bakers were adults capable of looking after themselves, and the hours limit had no direct relation to public health. Judgment reversed.

## Reasoning
The majority accepted that the states may regulate contracts under the police power, but insisted that the regulation have a real and substantial relation to health, safety, morals or welfare. Reviewing the evidence itself, the Court found the health argument too remote and concluded that the statute was in reality a labor regulation that favored one side of the bargain, which the Constitution does not permit. Justice Harlan's dissent would have deferred to the legislature given plausible evidence that long hours harmed bakers' health. Justice Holmes's dissent went further: the case was decided on an economic theory most of the country did not share, and a constitution is made for people of fundamentally differing views; the word liberty is perverted when used to block the natural outcome of a dominant opinion unless the law violates fundamental principles of our traditions.

## Rule (own words)
In its time, Lochner meant that courts would weigh for themselves whether an economic regulation was really needed. The modern rule is the reverse: economic and social legislation stands if any rational basis supports it, and courts reserve close scrutiny for laws that burden fundamental personal rights or target suspect classes.

## What-ifs
- If the law had applied only to minors or to a demonstrably hazardous trade such as mining, even the 1905 Court would likely have upheld it (it had upheld a miners' hours law in Holden v. Hardy).
- If the same law were challenged today, it would pass rational-basis review almost automatically.
- If a state today limited whom a person may marry or how a family may live, the Court would apply the stricter scrutiny Loving used: same clause, different kind of liberty.

## Class notes
Bring the discussion question written out: economic liberalism (freedom of contract, property, markets, minimal regulation) versus civil-rights liberalism (personal autonomy, equality, protection of minorities). Lochner protected the first and was repudiated; Loving protects the second and remains the model. The Mexican parallel: the 1917 Constitution went the other way from the start, constitutionalizing labor rights (art. 123) and social property (art. 27) while leaving economic freedom to the ordinary legislator.
