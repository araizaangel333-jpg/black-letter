## Facts
The defendant in a federal criminal case used a public generative AI tool on his own to analyze his situation and draft materials about his case, then shared some of the output with his lawyers. The government obtained the materials and the defendant claimed privilege and work product protection.

## Procedural posture
Motion practice before trial in the Southern District of New York; the court ruled the materials unprotected.

## Issue
Whether communications between a represented defendant and a generative AI tool, made without counsel's involvement, are protected by the attorney-client privilege or the work product doctrine.

## Holding
No. The defendant was not communicating with a lawyer for the purpose of obtaining legal advice, and the AI tool was not acting as the lawyers' agent; the documents were not prepared by or for counsel in anticipation of litigation in the sense the doctrine requires.

## Reasoning
Privilege attaches to the relationship with counsel, not to the subject matter of a document; a chatbot is a third party whose terms of service make the exchanges neither confidential nor part of the representation. The court noted the result might differ if the lawyers had directed the client to use the tool as part of their work, much as accountants or translators retained by counsel can fall inside the privilege.

## Rule (own words)
If you want AI work protected, counsel must own it: lawyers choose the tool, direct its use, and keep it inside the engagement. Anything a client types into a public chatbot on his own is discoverable.
