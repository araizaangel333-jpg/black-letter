## Facts
George Hawkins had a scarred palm from a childhood burn. Dr. McGee, who wanted to try skin grafting, told the boy's father he would guarantee to make the hand a hundred percent perfect. The graft, taken from the chest, left the hand hairy and worse than before.

## Procedural posture
Verdict for the plaintiff on the contract claim (the negligence claim failed). The trial judge found the damages excessive; on appeal the court held the jury had been misinstructed and ordered a new trial on damages.

## Issue
What the measure of damages is for breach of a promise to achieve a specific medical result.

## Holding
The difference between the value of a perfect hand, as promised, and the value of the hand after the operation, plus any incidental consequences within the parties' contemplation; not the pain of the operation and not the hand's value before surgery.

## Reasoning
The court analogized to a machine sold with a warranty: the buyer recovers the difference between the machine as warranted and as delivered. The patient bargained for a good hand at the cost of an operation; the breach is the shortfall in the result, and the damages must measure that shortfall.

## Rule (own words)
Contract damages look forward: what you were promised, minus what you got. What you agreed to suffer to get it is not part of the loss.

## What-ifs
- The surgeon merely said he hoped for a good result: no promise; only a negligence claim.
- The patient also lost wages because the hand was unusable: consequential damages if within contemplation.
- Sullivan v. O'Connor facts: a court may let the patient elect reliance damages (the pre-operation value, the fee and pain from unnecessary operations) when expectation is hard to prove.
