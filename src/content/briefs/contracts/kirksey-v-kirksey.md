## Facts
Antillico Kirksey, a widow with children, lived on public land she might have been able to keep. Her brother-in-law wrote that if she would come down and see him he would let her have a place to raise her family. She gave up her land, moved sixty miles with her children, and lived in a house on his land for two years. He then moved her to a worse house in the woods and later required her to leave.

## Procedural posture
She sued; the jury awarded $200; the Supreme Court of Alabama reversed, holding the promise unenforceable.

## Issue
Whether her move in response to the letter was consideration for the brother-in-law's promise.

## Holding
No. The promise was a mere gratuity; the move was a condition of receiving the gift, not something the promisor bargained for.

## Reasoning
The majority saw a kind relative offering a home, not a man buying a sister-in-law's relocation. Because the promisor sought nothing for himself, there was no exchange, and without exchange there is no consideration. The writing judge noted his own disagreement.

## Rule (own words)
Ask what the promisor wanted. If the promisee's act was the price, there is a bargain; if it was just the way to collect a gift, there is not.

## What-ifs
- The brother-in-law needed help farming and wrote: come work the land with me and I will give you a house. Bargain; consideration.
- Same facts today: promissory estoppel (R.2d § 90) likely enforces the promise to the extent of her reliance.
- She moved but had never received the letter and did not know of the promise: no reliance and no bargain; nothing.
