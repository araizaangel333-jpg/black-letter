## Facts
Elder & Johnston, a department store, advertised in a Dayton newspaper an electric sewing machine for $26, as a Thursday-only special. Craft went to the store on Thursday, tendered $26 and demanded the machine. The store refused to sell at that price.

## Procedural posture
Craft sued for damages. The trial court sustained a demurrer and dismissed; the court of appeals affirmed.

## Issue
Whether the advertisement was an offer that Craft's tender of the price accepted.

## Holding
No. The advertisement was an invitation to the public to make offers; Craft's tender was an offer the store declined.

## Reasoning
The court followed the general rule for advertisements: they are notices that goods may be bought at the store, not promises to sell to whoever shows up. Were it otherwise, a store could be bound to sell more machines than it had. Nothing in the ad's language committed the store to a particular buyer or limited the quantity in a way that would make the commitment definite.

## Rule (own words)
An ad opens the door; it does not close the deal. The customer makes the offer at the counter.

## What-ifs
- The ad read: one machine at $26, first come first served, Thursday 9 a.m. Offer under Lefkowitz; the first person to appear and tender accepts.
- Consumer-protection statutes (bait advertising) may give a remedy without any contract; that is regulation, not contract law.
- Mexican contrast: under the Ley Federal de Protección al Consumidor the advertised price binds the merchant toward consumers, a statutory rule that American contract law does not supply.
