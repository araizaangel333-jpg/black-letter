## Facts
In New Orleans in February 1815, Organ learned before the public that the Treaty of Ghent had ended the War of 1812, which meant the British blockade would lift and tobacco prices would jump. Early that morning he bought a large lot of tobacco from Laidlaw & Co. When the seller's agent asked whether there was any news affecting the price, Organ, by the record, said nothing. Once the news spread, Laidlaw refused to deliver and retook the tobacco.

## Procedural posture
Organ sued for the tobacco. The trial court directed a verdict for Organ. On writ of error, the Supreme Court (Marshall, C.J.) reversed and remanded for a new trial.

## Issue
Whether a buyer who knows of extrinsic news that will raise prices must disclose it to the seller before buying.

## Holding
No general duty to disclose, so long as the information was equally accessible to both sides; but the case had to go to a jury on whether Organ did anything to impose on the seller.

## Reasoning
Marshall reasoned that information "exclusively within the knowledge of the vendee" need not be shared when both parties had equal means of learning it; requiring disclosure would be hard to confine and would punish diligence. The limit is active deception: each party must take care not to say or do anything tending to impose upon the other. Because the trial judge had kept that question from the jury, a new trial was required.

## Rule (own words)
Silence about publicly discoverable market facts is not fraud. Active misleading is. R.2d § 161 later carved out specific situations where silence itself becomes an assertion.

## What-ifs
- If Organ had answered "no news" when asked, that would be a false assertion under R.2d § 159 and the contract would be voidable.
- If the parties had a relationship of trust (lawyer and client), R.2d § 161(d) would require disclosure.
