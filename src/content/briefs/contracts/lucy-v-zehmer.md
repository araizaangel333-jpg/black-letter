## Facts
At Zehmer's restaurant, after drinks, Lucy offered $50,000 for the Ferguson farm. Zehmer, who had refused earlier offers, wrote on the back of a guest check an agreement to sell the farm for $50,000, title satisfactory to buyer, tore it up and rewrote it to include his wife, and both Zehmers signed. Lucy offered $5 to bind the deal and later arranged financing and a title examination. The Zehmers then said the whole thing had been a joke to make Lucy admit he did not have $50,000.

## Procedural posture
Lucy sued for specific performance; the trial court denied relief; the Supreme Court of Appeals reversed and directed specific performance.

## Issue
Whether a contract was formed when one party privately intended a joke but his words and conduct indicated a serious agreement.

## Holding
Yes. The writing and the circumstances would lead a reasonable person to believe a real agreement was intended, Lucy did so believe, and the undisclosed intention to jest was immaterial.

## Reasoning
The law imputes to a person an intention corresponding to the reasonable meaning of his words and acts. The negotiation lasted long enough, the writing was careful enough and the follow-through serious enough that the court found a real agreement, or at least one Lucy was entitled to treat as real. Zehmer's intoxication did not reach the level of incapacity.

## Rule (own words)
You are bound by what you reasonably appear to agree to. If it looks like a deal and the other side takes it as one, it is one.

## What-ifs
- Lucy knew Zehmer was joking (for example, both laughed and tore up the check): no contract, because the objective test looks at what the other party knew or should have known.
- Zehmer was so drunk he could not understand what he signed and Lucy knew it: voidable for incapacity under R.2d § 16.
- The agreement was oral only: statute of frauds problem for a land sale, regardless of assent.
