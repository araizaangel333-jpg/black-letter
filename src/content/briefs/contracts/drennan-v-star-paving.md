## Facts
Drennan, a general contractor preparing a bid for a school project, received a telephone bid from Star Paving to do the paving for $7,131.60, the lowest of several. Drennan used it in his bid, which was awarded that evening. The next morning Star's engineer said the bid had been a mistake and it would not do the work for less than $15,000. Drennan hired another paver for $10,948.60 and sued for the difference.

## Procedural posture
Judgment for Drennan for $3,817; affirmed.

## Issue
Whether the subcontractor could revoke its bid after the general had relied on it, where no consideration had been given for an option and no acceptance preceded the revocation.

## Holding
No. The sub should reasonably have expected the general to rely on the bid, the general did, and injustice could be avoided only by treating the bid as irrevocable for a reasonable time; the general's prompt acceptance formed a contract and the sub was liable for the difference.

## Reasoning
Justice Traynor reasoned from § 90's protection of reliance: the sub had reason to know its bid would be used and wanted it used; having induced reliance, it could not withdraw without injustice, just as a promise induces enforceable reliance. The general had not bid-shopped or delayed and had no reason to know of the mistake, and the mistake was the sub's own.

## Rule (own words)
If you make a bid meant to be built into someone else's bid, you are stuck with it long enough for them to accept once they win, unless they played games or should have seen your mistake.

## What-ifs
- The general, after winning, calls other pavers to beat Star's price: bid-shopping; the option is lost.
- Star's bid was $1,000 for work obviously worth $10,000: the general should have known of the mistake; no reliance protection.
- The general waits a month after award and then accepts: unreasonable delay; the offer has lapsed.
- Star revokes before the general submits its bid: no reliance yet; revocation effective.
