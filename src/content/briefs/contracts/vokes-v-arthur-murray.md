## Facts
Audrey Vokes, a fifty-one-year-old widow, was told at a dance studio that she had grace and potential, and over several years was sold, through a stream of compliments, awards and assurances that she was progressing, fourteen contracts totaling 2,302 hours of lessons for more than $31,000. In fact she had little aptitude and the instructors knew it.

## Procedural posture
She sued to rescind; the trial court dismissed her amended complaint for failure to state a cause of action; the appellate court reversed.

## Issue
Whether statements of opinion about her dancing ability, made by the sellers to induce further purchases, could support a claim of misrepresentation.

## Holding
Yes. Where the parties were not on an equal footing and the sellers had superior knowledge, their opinions were representations she was entitled to rely on, and the complaint stated a cause of action.

## Reasoning
The general rule exempts opinions from the law of misrepresentation, but the exception applies when the speaker knows more and the listener reasonably relies on that knowledge; having undertaken to evaluate her, the studio had a duty not to mislead, and a half-truth that omits the facts needed to make it not misleading is as actionable as a lie.

## Rule (own words)
Experts who praise you to sell you something are making statements of fact about their own judgment; if they know better, it is misrepresentation.

## What-ifs
- A friend at a party told her she danced well and she bought lessons: opinion, no reliance justified, no claim.
- The studio told her she was a poor dancer but could improve with lessons: true opinion, no claim.
- Same facts under unconscionability (UCC § 2-302 by analogy): a court might also refuse to enforce the later contracts as oppressive.
