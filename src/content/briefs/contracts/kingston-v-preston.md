## Facts
Preston, a silk mercer, agreed to take Kingston as apprentice and, after a term, to transfer the business to Kingston and a nephew; Kingston in turn promised to give good and sufficient security for monthly payments for the stock. Kingston demanded the transfer without having provided the security; Preston refused.

## Procedural posture
Kingston sued; Preston pleaded the missing security; judgment for Preston.

## Issue
Whether Kingston could enforce the promise to transfer the business without having given the security he promised.

## Holding
No. The security was the essence of the agreement; the promise to transfer was dependent on it, so Kingston's failure excused Preston.

## Reasoning
Lord Mansfield rejected the argument that the promises were independent because each party could sue on the other's covenant: it would be absurd for Preston to hand over his stock to a man of no property in exchange for a lawsuit. The dependency of the covenants follows from the sense of the transaction.

## Rule (own words)
In an exchange, your right to the other side's performance depends on your own. First do, or offer to do, your part.

## What-ifs
- Both performances were due on the same day with nothing to be done first: concurrent conditions; each must tender to put the other in breach.
- The contract expressly said the transfer would occur regardless of the security: independent covenants by agreement; Kingston could sue, and Preston could sue separately.
- Kingston tendered the security a week late: question of material breach versus substantial performance, Jacob & Youngs territory.
