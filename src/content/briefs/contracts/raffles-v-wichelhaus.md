## Facts
The buyer agreed to buy cotton to arrive ex Peerless from Bombay. The seller shipped on a Peerless that sailed in December; the buyer claimed he meant a Peerless that sailed in October and refused the December cotton.

## Procedural posture
The seller sued; the buyer's plea of the misunderstanding was held good, so the seller lost.

## Issue
Whether a contract existed when each party meant a different ship by the same words.

## Holding
No. Parol evidence was admissible to show the latent ambiguity, and since the parties meant different ships, there was no consensus and no contract.

## Reasoning
The written term was clear on its face but ambiguous when applied to the facts (two ships). The court allowed proof of each party's meaning and, finding them different and equally plausible, held there was nothing to enforce.

## Rule (own words)
If the words fit two things equally well and each side honestly meant a different one, nobody agreed to anything.

## What-ifs
- The seller knew there were two ships and that the buyer meant the October one: the buyer's meaning governs under § 201(2); contract for October cotton; shipping December cotton is a breach.
- Trade usage in the Liverpool cotton market treated ex Peerless as the first ship sailing: usage resolves the ambiguity against whoever should have known it.
- The buyer's refusal was really about a price drop: still a defense if the misunderstanding was genuine; motive is irrelevant to assent.
