## Facts
Owen wrote to Tunison asking whether he would sell a commercial block in Bucksport, Maine, for $6,000. Tunison answered from France that because of improvements he had made it would not be possible for him to sell unless he received $16,000 cash. Owen replied accepting the property at $16,000 and asking for the deed. Tunison then wrote that he did not wish to sell.

## Procedural posture
Owen sued for breach of contract. The case was reported to the Supreme Judicial Court on the question whether a contract had been formed; the court held it had not.

## Issue
Whether Tunison's letter was an offer that Owen's acceptance could convert into a contract.

## Holding
No. The letter was at most a statement of the price below which the owner would not sell, an invitation to make an offer, and Owen's acceptance therefore formed no contract.

## Reasoning
The court applied the objective standard: an offer is a proposal that a reasonable person in the recipient's position would understand as inviting acceptance and promising to be bound. Tunison's language, that it would not be possible to sell unless he received $16,000, expressed a reluctance and a floor, not a commitment to convey at that price. Statements of price in reply to inquiries are ordinarily not offers.

## Rule (own words)
Look for commitment, not information. A seller who tells you the least he would take has told you where negotiations might start, not promised to sell.

## What-ifs
- Tunison writes: I will sell the block to you for $16,000 cash. Offer; Owen's letter accepts.
- Owen's first letter had said: I will pay $6,000; please accept by return mail. Then Owen made the offer, and Tunison's reply was a rejection and counter-statement.
- Tunison's letter was sent to several prospective buyers naming the same figure: even stronger that it was an invitation, since one cannot sell one block to all.
