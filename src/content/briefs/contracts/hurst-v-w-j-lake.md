## Facts
Hurst sold W.J. Lake & Co. a quantity of horse meat scraps under a contract calling for "minimum 50% protein," with a price reduction of $5 per ton for scraps testing below 50%. Several lots tested between 49.53% and 49.96%. The buyer took the reduction; the seller said that in the trade "50% protein" meant anything at or above 49.5%, so no reduction was owed.

## Procedural posture
The seller sued for the unpaid balance. The trial court held the words unambiguous, excluded the evidence of trade usage and entered judgment for the buyer. The seller appealed to the Oregon Supreme Court.

## Issue
Whether evidence of a usage of trade may be used to show that "minimum 50% protein" meant 49.5% or more to parties dealing in horse meat scraps, even though the words look clear on their face.

## Holding
Yes. Reversed. The usage evidence is admissible, because words take their meaning from the trade in which both parties operate.

## Reasoning
Language is not self-defining; "plain meaning" is itself a conclusion about what the parties meant. Where both parties belong to a trade with a settled usage, the usage is the dictionary they were using, and a court that ignores it rewrites the bargain. The buyer's argument that the number is mathematically exact proves too much: trades routinely use numbers with tolerances. The losing argument (the parol evidence rule bars changing the writing) fails because the usage does not contradict the writing; it explains it.

## Rule (own words)
A usage of trade known to both parties supplies the meaning of contract terms, and a court must admit evidence of it even when the words seem unambiguous. Modern codification: UCC § 1-303(c), (d) and R.2d § 222.

## What-ifs
- If the usage contradicted an express term outright, the express term would control (UCC § 1-303(e)).
- If one party were outside the trade, the usage binds only if that party knew or had reason to know of it.
