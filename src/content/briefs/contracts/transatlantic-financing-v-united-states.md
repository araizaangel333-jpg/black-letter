## Facts
Transatlantic chartered a ship to the United States to carry wheat from Texas to Iran. After the voyage began, Egypt nationalized the Suez Canal and, following the Israeli and Anglo-French attacks, closed it. The ship sailed around the Cape of Good Hope, adding about 3,000 miles and roughly $44,000 to a $306,000 contract. The owner delivered and then sued for the added expense.

## Procedural posture
Dismissed; affirmed.

## Issue
Whether the canal's closure rendered performance by the usual route impracticable so as to excuse the owner and entitle it to the extra cost.

## Holding
No. Even assuming the Suez route was the contemplated route, the risk of its closure was not allocated to the government, and the Cape route was not commercially impracticable; the owner performed its contract and could recover only the contract price.

## Reasoning
Judge Wright set out the three-part test and applied it: the closure was unexpected enough; but the owner, more able to foresee and insure against the risk, could not shift it to the charterer by silence; and the alternative was merely more expensive, not so different in kind that performance became impracticable. The court also rejected a restitution claim layered on top of the contract price.

## Rule (own words)
Harder and dearer is not impossible. Unless the contract or custom put the risk on the other side, you perform by the alternative and eat the cost.

## What-ifs
- The charter specified via Suez and the canal closed: route is a term; closure may excuse or justify a renegotiation, but the owner still must perform by the alternative if feasible unless the contract says otherwise.
- Cost increased tenfold because of war-zone insurance: closer to impracticability; the case says the amount matters but gives no number.
- Mexican comparison: teoría de la imprevisión is recognized in some state codes (not the CCF for the federal district historically), allowing adjustment; American law prefers excuse or nothing.
