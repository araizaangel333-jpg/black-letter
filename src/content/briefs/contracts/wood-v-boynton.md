## Facts
Mrs. Wood found a small stone and showed it to Boynton, a jeweler, who said he did not know what it was but would give a dollar for it; she accepted. The stone was an uncut diamond worth about $700. She tendered the dollar back and demanded the stone.

## Procedural posture
She sued to recover the stone; verdict directed for the jeweler; affirmed.

## Issue
Whether the seller could rescind for mutual mistake about the stone's nature and value.

## Holding
No. There was no fraud, no warranty and no mistake as to the identity of the thing sold; both knew its nature was uncertain and the seller chose to sell for a dollar.

## Reasoning
The court held that the only mistake was as to value, which is no ground for rescission; the seller could have kept the stone and investigated. Having sold a thing she knew she could not identify, she took the chance that it was worth more.

## Rule (own words)
Selling something you both know you cannot identify is a bet, and the loser of a bet does not get a refund.

## What-ifs
- The jeweler recognized it as a diamond and said it was probably a topaz: misrepresentation; rescission.
- Both parties affirmatively believed it was a topaz because an assayer had told them so: closer to Sherwood; mutual mistake about a basic assumption.
- Mrs. Wood was a child: capacity defense, voidable regardless of mistake.
