## Facts
Dodds delivered a written offer to sell houses to Dickinson for £800, with a postscript that the offer was to be left over until Friday at 9 a.m. On Thursday afternoon Dickinson decided to accept but did not say so at once. That evening he learned from Berry, who had been helping him, that Dodds had been negotiating or had agreed to sell to Allan. Dickinson then left a written acceptance at Dodds's house, and the next morning before 9 handed him another copy; Dodds said he had already sold.

## Procedural posture
Dickinson sued for specific performance against Dodds and Allan; the Vice-Chancellor granted it; the Court of Appeal reversed and dismissed.

## Issue
Whether Dodds could revoke before Friday, and whether the offer was revoked by Dickinson's learning of the sale to Allan.

## Holding
Yes to both. The promise to hold the offer open was not binding for want of consideration, and once Dickinson knew Dodds had sold to Allan, there was no longer any offer for him to accept.

## Reasoning
There was no contract until acceptance, and the postscript was only a statement of intention not to withdraw, which Dodds was free to change. A formal notice of withdrawal is unnecessary where the offeree actually knows the offeror has changed his mind; one cannot accept an offer one knows is gone.

## Rule (own words)
Unless you paid to keep it open, an offer is open only while the offeror says so, and knowing he sold to someone else is being told.

## What-ifs
- Dickinson paid £1 for the promise to hold until Friday: option contract; the sale to Allan would be a breach, and specific performance against Dodds (and perhaps Allan with notice) could follow.
- Dickinson accepted Thursday morning before any sale: contract formed; later sale breaches it.
- Berry's information was a rumor from a stranger: not reliable; the offer may still stand.
