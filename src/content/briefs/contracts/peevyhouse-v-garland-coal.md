## Facts
The Peevyhouses leased their farm to Garland for strip mining, insisting on a clause requiring the company to restore the land (fill pits, grade) at the end of the lease. The company mined and left without doing the work, which would have cost about $29,000; the restoration would have increased the farm's market value by about $300.

## Procedural posture
Jury verdict of $5,000; the Supreme Court of Oklahoma reduced it to $300.

## Issue
Whether damages are the cost of the promised restoration or the diminution in the land's value.

## Holding
Diminution in value. The restoration clause was incidental to the main purpose of the lease (mining), and the cost of completion was grossly disproportionate to the benefit.

## Reasoning
The majority relied on the rule against economic waste and on cases (and a Restatement section) limiting damages where completion costs far exceed the value gained; it reasoned that the parties could not have intended the company to pay $29,000 to add $300 of value. The dissent would have enforced the bargain the landowners expressly made.

## Rule (own words)
If fixing what was promised costs far more than it is worth and the promise was not the heart of the deal, you get the drop in value. Expect a fight about which promises are the heart of the deal.

## What-ifs
- The contract said: the restoration is of the essence and the lessee agrees to pay its full cost. Express allocation; cost of completion.
- The land was the family homestead with evidence of personal value: subjective value may justify cost of completion under § 348 comment.
- The company's breach was willful and it saved $29,000: Groves-type reasoning may award cost of completion to deny the windfall.
