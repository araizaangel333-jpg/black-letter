## Facts
The bank sold Swinton a house it knew was infested with termites, a fact not discoverable by ordinary inspection. The bank said nothing about it. Swinton discovered the infestation after the sale and spent money on repairs and treatment.

## Procedural posture
Swinton sued for damages for fraudulent concealment; the court sustained the bank's demurrer and the Supreme Judicial Court affirmed.

## Issue
Whether a seller's failure to disclose a known, material, latent defect is actionable fraud.

## Holding
No. The declaration alleged no false statement, no half-truth and no act of concealment, only silence; the law imposed no duty to speak, and the buyer bore the risk.

## Reasoning
The court reasoned that a duty to disclose every known defect would have no stopping point: the same rule would bind buyers who know facts raising the value of what they buy. Without a fiduciary relationship or an affirmative act, the parties deal at arm's length and each may keep what he knows.

## Rule (own words)
Silence is safe at common law; words, half-words and cover-ups are not. Check whether any exception creates a duty to speak before calling silence fraud.

## What-ifs
- The bank's agent said the house had been treated for termites and was fine: false assertion; misrepresentation.
- The bank filled the holes and painted the beams: active concealment; equivalent to an assertion.
- A Texas home seller today: statutory disclosure notice required (Tex. Prop. Code § 5.008), so the result changes by statute.
