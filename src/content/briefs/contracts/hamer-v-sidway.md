## Facts
At a family celebration, William Story promised his nephew, William Story 2d, that if the nephew refrained from drinking liquor, using tobacco, swearing and playing cards or billiards for money until he turned twenty-one, the uncle would pay him $5,000. The nephew complied. After he came of age he wrote to the uncle, who replied that the money was his but that the uncle would hold it for him at interest. The uncle died without paying. The nephew's rights passed by assignment to Hamer, who sued the executor, Sidway.

## Procedural posture
Judgment for the claimant at trial; reversed at the General Term; the Court of Appeals reversed that and affirmed the trial judgment.

## Issue
Whether the nephew's abstention was consideration for the uncle's promise, given that abstaining benefited the nephew and cost the uncle nothing.

## Holding
Yes. The nephew had a legal right to drink and smoke; abandoning that right at the uncle's request was a detriment that supplied consideration, and the uncle's promise was enforceable.

## Reasoning
The court adopted the classic definition of consideration as a benefit to the promisor or a detriment to the promisee, and refused to inquire whether the forbearance actually benefited the nephew. The promise induced the forbearance and the forbearance was the price of the promise. The court also dealt with the statute of limitations by treating the uncle's letter as creating a trust of the money.

## Rule (own words)
Giving up something you were free to do, because someone promised to pay you for it, is consideration. The court does not ask whether the deal was wise for you.

## What-ifs
- The uncle promised the money if the nephew would stop using heroin: no consideration, because the nephew had no legal right to do it (no detriment).
- The uncle promised $5,000 as a gift at the party and the nephew later abstained on his own: no bargain, so no consideration (compare Kirksey).
- The nephew abstained but the uncle had died before any promise reached him: no promise, no claim.
