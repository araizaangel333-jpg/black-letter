## Facts
Corlies & Tift negotiated with White, a builder, about fitting up a suite of offices. After some exchanges, their bookkeeper left White a note: upon an agreement to finish the fitting up of the offices in two weeks from date, you can begin at once. White said nothing to them but bought lumber and began working on it in his shop. The next day Corlies & Tift sent a countermand. White sued.

## Procedural posture
The trial judge told the jury that if White began work on the lumber with the intention of accepting, that was acceptance. Verdict for White; the Court of Appeals reversed and ordered a new trial.

## Issue
Whether White's purchase of lumber and start of work in his own shop, uncommunicated to the offerors, accepted the offer before it was withdrawn.

## Holding
No. The offer called for an agreement to finish in two weeks, which required some manifestation of assent; White's acts were not of a character to indicate assent to the offerors and were never brought to their attention, so the countermand came before any acceptance.

## Reasoning
The court accepted that assent may be shown by acts as well as words, but the acts must be such as would indicate acceptance to the other party, and they must be made known. Buying lumber and working on it in one's own shop is what a builder does for any customer; it tells the offeror nothing. An intention to accept, however sincere, is not an acceptance.

## Rule (own words)
Accepting is something you do toward the other side, not something you feel. Either tell them or do something they will see that could only mean yes.

## What-ifs
- White sends a boy with the message I accept before the countermand arrives: contract.
- White begins tearing out the old fixtures at the offices on the morning after the note: an act referable to the offer that the offerors would discover; likely acceptance.
- The note had said: send us word within a day if you agree. Then only a communication accepts.
