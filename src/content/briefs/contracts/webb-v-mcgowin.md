## Facts
Webb, working in a lumber mill, was dropping a heavy pine block from an upper floor when he saw McGowin below. To divert the block he fell with it, saving McGowin from death or serious injury and crippling himself for life. McGowin promised to pay Webb $15 every two weeks for the rest of Webb's life and paid for about eight years until his death; the executors then stopped.

## Procedural posture
Webb sued the executors; the trial court sustained a demurrer; the Court of Appeals reversed and the Supreme Court denied certiorari, approving the result.

## Issue
Whether a promise made after the promisor received a material benefit, in recognition of that benefit, is supported by consideration.

## Holding
Yes. Saving a life and avoiding injury is a material benefit; the subsequent promise to compensate the rescuer for his detriment is enforceable, and the law implies a request.

## Reasoning
The court reasoned that if the promisor would have been liable in quasi-contract for a benefit he requested, his later promise to pay for a benefit he actually received should likewise be enforced, with the promise fixing the amount; the moral obligation plus the material benefit supplied what a bare moral obligation lacks.

## Rule (own words)
When you are the one who got the benefit and you then promise to pay for it, the promise sticks. Mills fails only because the father got nothing himself.

## What-ifs
- Webb had promised the rescue as a gift and expected nothing: no enforcement under § 86(2)(a).
- McGowin promised $1 million a year: enforceable only to a proportionate extent under § 86(2)(b).
- The promise was made before the rescue (I will pay you if you push the block away): ordinary bargain, no need for the exception.
