## What the course is
A two-credit Friday seminar (9:45–11:45, Florence Hall 106; office hours Thursdays 4–5 by Zoom, scheduled by email) that introduces the U.S. legal system through the Constitution and a handful of Supreme Court cases, and that draws constantly on the students' own systems. The syllabus lists four outcomes: say what the common law is and how it relates to American law and mores; use the basic study techniques of U.S. legal education; understand, interpret and apply constitutional doctrines and cases; and see the contrasts with your home country. The use of AI is encouraged, including in class.

## How you are graded
- Final exam: comprehensive and in class, date to be announced. The professor has flagged Loving v. Virginia as final exam material, which suggests the cases discussed in class are fair game, not only the book.
- Assignments: all must be completed to be allowed to sit for the final.
- Participation: required and part of the grade; attendance is taken.
- The midterm (October 9) was ten essay-form questions of ten points each on Examify, taken from home, with notes, books and AI allowed. Expect the final to reward the same thing: clear essays that explain a doctrine, apply it to the facts and compare it with your country.

## The weekly routine that fits this class
1. Monday or Tuesday (phone): read the announcement for the week; the professor often amends the reading on Wednesday or Thursday, so check again Thursday night.
2. Wednesday (laptop, 45 minutes): read the assigned pages and brief the assigned case using the templates here; add three glossary terms.
3. Thursday (phone, 10 minutes): write the five-line Mexico answer to the week's discussion question and your "if called on, my first sentence is" line.
4. Friday after class (phone, 5 minutes): capture three lines: the rule we actually used, the case we spent longest on, one thing I did not understand.
5. Sunday: ten minutes of the quiz set for the topic, and move anything unclear into a question for office hours.

## The discussion questions so far, in order
Your country's legal system; whether and how it divides powers; how people become judges; your supreme court; federalism; flag burning as a crime; whether courts are agents of societal change; LGBT rights; and, for October 30, what distinguishes liberalism in the economic sense from liberalism in the civil-rights sense. Keep one paragraph on each in your outline; the final will likely ask for the comparison.

## Extras the professor recommended
Revolutionary Summer by Joseph Ellis for the founding period; Spielberg's Lincoln for citizenship and the Thirteenth Amendment; the HBO documentary The Loving Story, shown in class on October 30.
